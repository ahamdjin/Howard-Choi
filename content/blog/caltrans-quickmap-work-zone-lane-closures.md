## Start with the part of the trip you actually need

Say you are leaving Buena Park for an appointment in Los Angeles. Someone tells you there is work on I-5 tonight. That information could mean a single lane is closed, the entrance you use is closed, or every through lane in one direction is closed. Each calls for a different plan. Before reaching for a different route, identify the direction you will travel, where you expect to enter, and which connector or exit you need.

Open [Caltrans QuickMap](https://quickmap.dot.ca.gov/) while you are parked or before you leave. Find the highway and zoom until you can distinguish the two directions and nearby ramps. A marker beside an interchange may describe a connector with a different route number from the main freeway. Read its details before deciding whether it affects your trip. Someone traveling east on SR-91 may see the same marker as someone joining from I-5 and reach a different, equally sensible conclusion.

Keep your destination in mind. If you just need to know whether an entrance ramp is open, begin there. Looking at every camera, incident icon and traffic color across Orange County at once can make a straightforward question surprisingly hard to answer. QuickMap describes conditions on the state highway system. If construction is on a neighborhood street, check the city’s own notices for that street.

## Turn on the layers that answer your question

For road work, start with lane closures and full closures. Open the marker and look for the road, direction, affected segment and available time information. Then check the traffic-speed layer if you want a sense of the queue. A red line describes slow traffic; it does not identify the reason. An orange lane-closure marker gives different information. Slow traffic can extend well beyond the work, and a closure can be in effect before a visible queue forms.

CHP incident markers can help if the slowdown may involve a collision or another reported hazard. Read the location and time in the entry. A crash and scheduled maintenance can affect the same stretch of road without one causing the other. When a closure and a collision appear close together, avoid treating the two pins as proof of what happened between them. Caltrans explains what each symbol represents in its [QuickMap guide to layers and updates](https://quickmap.dot.ca.gov/faq).

When the screen feels crowded, switch off the layers you do not need, zoom in and open one item at a time. Read the freeway name inside the detail window. At a stacked interchange, the marker nearest your blue location dot may sit on a roadway above or below yours. Use the named direction and ramp to identify the movement described.

## Today’s map and tomorrow’s planned closure answer different questions

A construction notice can describe work planned for a time window. QuickMap can help you understand what is being reported now. If you are organizing a trip for later in the week, use the planned lane-closure information linked from the Caltrans FAQ. Then check again close to departure. A crew may finish early, conditions may change, or the work may affect only one direction at a time.

For an overnight trip, pay special attention to the date change. An appointment shortly after midnight belongs to the next calendar day, while the notice may give the start time from the evening before. Write down both ends of the interval if someone else will drive you. “Tuesday night after dinner through Wednesday morning” can be less ambiguous in a message than “tomorrow at twelve.”

If you are arranging transportation for a medical visit, it may be enough to tell the person collecting you that the usual ramp has a planned closure and agree on an alternate pickup in advance. A clinic address and appointment time still matter if a driver changes. Share those directly. A screenshot with a traffic icon and no destination is difficult for the next driver to interpret.

## An example at an Orange County interchange

Imagine a passenger checking the route before a trip from Buena Park. The map shows an orange cone near a freeway interchange and red traffic along the approach. The passenger opens the cone and sees that it concerns the ramp they planned to use. The red line continues beyond it. They can tell the driver which ramp is affected and that the approach appears slow. They cannot tell how long a particular driver has been waiting, or whether the ramp work caused the entire queue.

Now change one fact. Suppose the closure applies to the opposite direction. The red traffic on the intended route still deserves attention, but the closure entry no longer explains it. Check for another reported incident and allow more time if needed. If you share the notice with someone, include the direction in the message. Two people can accurately say “I saw construction on the map” while describing completely different trips.

Once the trip begins, leave phone checks to a passenger or wait until safely parked. Follow the signs, signals, flaggers and lane markings you encounter on the road. A map viewed at home cannot tell you whether a crew has just shifted a taper or whether a temporary sign is now directing your lane elsewhere. Use a navigation service separately if you need turn-by-turn directions. Caltrans describes QuickMap as a road-conditions resource.

## If a crash happens near the work zone

Start with immediate safety and any needed medical assistance. Once safe, record the highway, direction, approximate time and nearest exit or ramp name. A tow invoice can identify where a vehicle was collected. If the driver moved it to a turnout after impact, say so. The collection point may be much easier to identify than the spot where the vehicles first touched.

Think about what you saw before the collision. Were there advance warnings? Was an arrow board directing traffic away from a lane? Where did the line of stopped vehicles begin relative to the work? A narrowing lane can affect visibility well before drivers reach the cones. If a witness saw the queue form but did not see impact, that account can still help explain the approach. The [California lane-change crash guide](/blogs/unsafe-lane-change-accident-fault-california) covers the separate question of how vehicles moved as drivers merged.

If debris, a sign, or equipment appears relevant, describe its position and anything identifying it that you could see from safety. A logo on a nearby truck does not by itself establish who placed a sign or controlled the segment. Keep the original photo if one exists. When speaking with an insurer, distinguish “I saw a barrier at the right edge” from “the contractor put the barrier in my lane.” The first is an observation. The second may require work plans, witnesses or other records. If a commercial truck was involved in the collision, the [truck accident practice guide](/practice-areas/truck-accidents) explains which records can help establish its movement through the work zone.

## What a screenshot can document

A screenshot can preserve the notice you actually read. If you already have one, keep the original file with its timestamp. A useful capture shows the open detail panel, route and direction, the surrounding interchange, and any update time displayed. If the close-up cuts off the route name, a second, wider view can supply the context. Keep personal addresses or device notifications out of copies you share publicly.

Record when you took it and when the incident happened. For example, “map checked at home at 7:10 a.m.; passenger says collision occurred around 8 a.m.” is more precise than labeling the file “conditions at impact.” A marker may have changed in between. QuickMap layers refresh at different intervals, and camera images can update at different times. A screenshot taken after the fact can help identify whom to ask for records without settling what drivers saw earlier.

For the same reason, an empty stretch of map has limits. It tells you that the selected view showed no relevant marker when you looked. Ask whether the right layer was on, whether you selected the right direction, and whether the road belongs to the state highway system. An absent marker cannot establish that no sign, closure, incident or temporary hazard existed.

## Which document to request next

Match the document to the question. If you need a record of the collision itself, identify the investigating agency and report number. If you need to understand a planned closure, save its identifying details and the name of the agency that issued it. If the dispute is about what happened to the vehicles, ask about witness accounts, dashcam footage and photographs showing the approach. The [California crash-report correction guide](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) can help if the agency’s report later names a different ramp or direction.

When assembling records for a [car accident consultation](/practice-areas/car-accidents), bring the notice, the original capture and a short explanation of why they matter. Someone reviewing a week of construction alerts may struggle to find the one that applied. A note identifying “northbound approach, before the connector, approximately 6 p.m.” lets them start with the right segment and ask a more useful follow-up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I look at QuickMap the night before a trip?

Yes. Use the planned-closure resource for the scheduled window and check current conditions before leaving. If your route includes an entrance, connector and exit on different highways, check each movement. A through lane can remain open while the exit you need is unavailable.

### Does a traffic camera let me replay a collision?

A camera snapshot on QuickMap is a view of a place at a particular time. Its presence does not establish that a recording of your collision is available. Note the camera location and direction if relevant and promptly ask the agency responsible about any records it retains. Preserve independent video when available.

### Does a crash marker tell me who was at fault?

No. A reported incident can help identify a location and time to investigate. It cannot establish what a driver saw, whether someone had room to merge or which party was responsible. Those questions need evidence specific to the collision.

### Can someone check the map for me after a crash?

A passenger or someone at home can check safely. Ask them to save what they saw and the time they looked. If they did not witness the crash, keep their map observation separate from the accounts of people who were there.
