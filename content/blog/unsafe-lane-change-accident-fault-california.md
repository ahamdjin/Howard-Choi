## What California’s unsafe lane change rule asks

California Vehicle Code section 22107 addresses moving right or left from a direct course. A driver must be able to make the movement with reasonable safety and must give an appropriate signal when another vehicle may be affected. Turning on a blinker tells people what a driver intends to do. It does not reserve the adjacent lane or establish that there was enough space to enter it. [California Vehicle Code section 22107](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=22107.&lawCode=VEH)

For a claim, the practical question is what happened during the seconds before contact. Which lanes were occupied? What part of each vehicle crossed the line? Was either vehicle accelerating, slowing or moving around an obstruction? Could the drivers reasonably see one another? The answers may point strongly toward one driver, or they may leave room for more than one person’s conduct to have contributed.

| Disputed question | Evidence that may help | Limit to keep in mind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which vehicle moved first? | Continuous video, a passenger’s account or a witness with a view of both lanes | A still photo after impact may show only where vehicles stopped |
| Was the adjacent lane occupied? | A diagram, video of the approach and testimony about relative positions | A driver may have seen the impact without seeing the earlier approach |
| Did a signal give warning? | Footage showing a lamp, reflections or an independent observation | A lamp visible after contact does not establish when it came on |
| Did a lane end or continue? | Markings, signs, construction layout and dated photographs | A later map may reflect a different configuration |

## Draw the road before drawing conclusions

Begin with a blank sheet. Add the direction of travel, the nearest intersection or freeway exit and the number of through lanes. Mark turn-only pockets, merging ramps, painted islands and temporary cones. Number the lanes from a stated reference point. Two people can both say “the second lane” while counting from opposite sides. A small arrow showing north or the direction of travel makes the rest of the drawing easier to read.

Use separate marks for where you first noticed the other vehicle, where contact occurred and where you eventually stopped. On a busy Orange County freeway, the nearest safe stopping place may be beyond an exit or several lanes from the impact. A photograph taken at that stopping place can document damage and people present. Label it as the stopping location so a reviewer does not treat the shoulder in the photograph as the point of impact.

If your own diagram is approximate, say so. A description such as “the white sedan was near my passenger door when I first noticed it” is useful without a guessed distance or speed. Record what you saw in a mirror separately from what you saw through a window. If you heard the impact before you saw the other car, explain that sequence. It is possible to give an accurate account with a gap in it.

## How three familiar crashes can lead to different questions

On a freeway on-ramp, a driver approaching the end of the acceleration lane may believe another motorist deliberately closed a gap. The through-driver may say the merging vehicle moved over too soon. Useful evidence includes how long the vehicles traveled side by side, where the lane markings changed and whether there was traffic immediately ahead. Simply describing the road as a merge leaves all three questions open.

On a surface street, a driver may move around a stopped delivery truck. The other vehicle could be traveling steadily in the next lane or pulling out of a driveway. Write down the position of the delivery truck, whether it obscured a view and where the driveway entered the road. If the company name is visible, record it from a safe place. A delivery driver who never felt the collision may still be able to explain why the truck was there and what the surrounding lanes looked like.

A third situation involves two vehicles entering the middle lane from opposite sides. Each driver may have checked an empty space before both began moving. The useful questions become timing, sightlines and how far each vehicle had entered the lane. A witness behind both cars or a camera facing the roadway may see what neither driver could see from inside. The same section of the Vehicle Code can be relevant to more than one movement.

## Preserve a few seconds more than you think you need

A dashboard camera can overwrite a recording during the drive home. When it is safe to do so, protect the relevant file and preserve the original format. Save the approach before the impact and the sequence afterward. A short clip beginning with a loud sound may capture the consequence while omitting the signal, the earlier lane positions or an obstruction that caused someone to move.

Note whether the camera clock matched local time. If a clock was several minutes fast, keep the original file and document the difference rather than modifying its timestamp. If you provide a trimmed copy for convenience, keep the full original. Save rear-facing footage if the vehicle has it. A front camera may show where the road was going, while a rear camera may show a vehicle approaching from a lane that the front camera never captured.

For nearby businesses, note the address and which side of the building faces the incident. Ask promptly whether recordings exist and whether the business can preserve the relevant time window. A visible camera may point at a doorway or may not retain footage. Treat preservation and obtaining a copy as separate steps. A store might preserve a recording but release it only through its own process. Never enter a traffic lane or private property to look for a better angle.

## What side damage can and cannot tell you

Photograph the whole car before taking close-ups, if conditions allow. Include the front and rear corners on the damaged side, the wheels and the other vehicle when it can be done safely. A close-up of transferred paint is difficult to interpret when nobody can tell which panel it came from. Keep the repair estimate with the photographs so a later reviewer can match the damaged area to the appropriate vehicle.

A scrape along a passenger door may be consistent with lateral contact. On its own, it does not establish which vehicle crossed the lane line. The driver who was moving can end up with damage on either side, depending on travel direction and relative position. Earlier damage, later contact with a barrier and movement after the initial impact can also complicate the picture. Where the accounts materially disagree, preserve the vehicles’ condition and ask about inspection before repairs or disposal.

## If the collision report gets a lane wrong

Read the diagram and narrative together. Check the direction of travel, the stated number of lanes, each vehicle’s identifier and any witness quotation attributed to you. A reversed vehicle number can make an otherwise accurate diagram look like an admission by the wrong driver. Mark the specific entry that concerns you and identify the photograph or statement that explains the discrepancy.

Keep the original report. Ask the investigating agency about its procedure for submitting a supplemental statement or correction and save the submission date. An agency may accept factual material without changing the officer’s opinion. The [California collision report correction guide](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) walks through that distinction. If no report was prepared, the [guide to claims without a police report](/blogs/car-insurance-claim-without-police-report-california) covers other ways to document the event.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the driver changing lanes always entirely at fault?

Responsibility depends on the evidence. An unsafe movement can be important, but another driver’s conduct and the physical layout can matter too. Identify the actions that allegedly contributed and what supports each one. California’s [comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) explains how a share of responsibility can affect a claim when more than one person contributed to the harm.

### What if the other driver says I was in a blind spot?

Ask what position the vehicles occupied and for how long. A blind spot can help explain why someone did not notice a vehicle. It does not answer whether the driver could safely move over. The [DMV guide to navigating California roads](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/navigating-the-roads/) describes the observations drivers should make before changing lanes.

### Can a witness help if they did not see the impact?

Yes, if their observations address a disputed fact. A person who saw the approach might describe which lane a car occupied or an obstruction that blocked a view. Record their contact information and distinguish what they actually observed from anything someone told them after the collision. Let witnesses describe the event in their own words.

### Does an insurer’s percentage settle who was legally responsible?

An adjuster’s allocation states the company’s position. Ask for its explanation and what evidence would address the point in dispute. Preserve any proposed agreement or release before responding. The [insurance adjuster guide](/blogs/what-is-an-insurance-adjuster-car-accident) explains how to identify who is evaluating the claim and how to keep a record of the conversation.

### What should I bring to a first conversation about a sideswipe?

Bring the original footage, photos labeled by vehicle, the collision report if available, witness details and your diagram. Include insurer correspondence, repair information and records of injury or missed work if relevant. If you have been discharged and are unsure whom to call next, see [how to arrange follow-up care after a crash](/blogs/what-doctor-to-see-after-car-accident-california). Flag any recording that might soon be erased. The [Buena Park car accident page](/practice-areas/car-accidents) explains how those records fit into the wider review of an injury claim.
