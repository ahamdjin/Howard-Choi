## What the posted speed limit tells you

Picture a driver approaching a familiar intersection on Beach Boulevard. The signal ahead is green. Yesterday the pavement was dry and the intersection was empty. Today a bus is stopped at the curb, a delivery truck blocks the view of a crosswalk, and rain has turned the lane markings into pale reflections. The speed limit sign gives the same number on both trips. The distance the driver can see clearly has changed.

California Vehicle Code section 22350 directs drivers to consider weather, visibility, traffic, and the surface and width of the highway. The rule also protects people and property from a speed that creates danger. The [California Driver’s Handbook](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/safe-driving-cont2/) describes how those conditions affect the choice of speed. A posted maximum still applies; good weather and an open road do not give a driver permission to exceed it.

After a collision, the question is easier to understand if you start a few seconds before impact. What could the driver reasonably see? Where was there space to slow down? When did that space disappear? An answer such as “traffic was moving, but I could only see as far as the back of the bus” gives an investigator more to work with than “everyone was doing the limit.”

## Rain, glare and the road around the next bend

Rain changes both what a driver can see and how the tires meet the road. Glare can hide a pedestrian even on a dry afternoon. A bend can conceal a queue of stopped cars until the driver is almost on it. Those are different problems. A photograph of wet pavement may help describe the first one, while a view looking into the setting sun may better explain the second.

Suppose two drivers disagree about a crash near a curve. One remembers that the road was wet; the other says the rain had stopped. Both may be describing what they saw accurately. The useful follow-up is whether standing water remained on the approach, how far ahead traffic was visible, and what happened as the vehicles entered the curve. If a car slid, record when the driver first felt the slide and which way the car moved. The [guide to skids and tire grip](/blogs/car-accidents-what-is-a-skid-and-how-can-you-avoid-them) explains why a vehicle’s path can change even while its wheels are turned.

Conditions can also change within the length of a block. Bright sun on the approach can give way to shade beneath an overpass. Water can collect along one edge of an otherwise dry lane. Ask a witness which part of the street they saw. Someone standing under an awning may have had a clear view of the impact and no useful view of the preceding bend.

## When traffic is moving at two different speeds

On a freeway approaching Buena Park, the through lanes may be moving freely while vehicles in an exit lane are slowing to a crawl. A driver who judges speed only by the traffic on the left may miss the end of the queue on the right. A driver entering from an on-ramp may be concentrating on an opening in fast traffic when the opening closes. In either situation, “the freeway was clear” leaves out the lane that mattered.

Write down the lane each vehicle occupied, whether its brake lights were visible, and where the queue began relative to a sign or ramp. If another driver changed lanes, describe when the front of that vehicle crossed the line and how much room remained. There may be a question about both the lane change and the following driver’s response. The [unsafe lane change guide](/blogs/unsafe-lane-change-accident-fault-california) explains what to preserve when the accounts differ.

The Basic Speed Law is sometimes confused with California’s rule about driving so slowly that traffic is impeded. They address different concerns. Slowing for a visible hazard can be appropriate even when a line forms behind you. If the dispute is about a vehicle moving unusually slowly on a clear road, start with the [California minimum speed guide](/blogs/californias-minimum-speed-law/). Tell the person reviewing the claim why the vehicle slowed, including any hazard that was hidden from the driver behind it.

## How to describe another vehicle’s speed fairly

A person looking out from a driveway may remember that a car covered the space between two parked vehicles very quickly. That observation has value even if the witness could not reliably estimate miles per hour. Ask where the person stood, which landmarks they remember, and whether they watched the vehicle continuously. Keep their own words. A later estimate repeated by several people can begin to sound more certain than anyone’s original observation.

There is a similar problem with a passenger’s account. “I saw 32 on the dashboard when we passed the gas station” is different from “we were doing 32 at impact.” The passenger may have looked down only once. Record the location of the observation and what happened afterward. If the driver braked, turned or moved into another lane between those moments, an investigator needs to know that too.

A still image rarely settles speed by itself. Even video can be misleading if the time display is wrong, the frame rate changed during export, or the camera looks down the street at an angle. Preserve the original recording and a longer period before impact. A clipped video shared through a messaging app is convenient to watch, but keep the original file and device details when available so a qualified reviewer can interpret the timing.

## Which records can answer a disputed speed question?

Start with the piece of the story that is disputed. If one party says a pedestrian stepped out from behind a stopped truck, the location and size of the truck may matter more than a close-up of the bumper. If both drivers agree there was a queue but disagree about when it became visible, a view looking back along the approach may explain the disagreement. Ask someone else to take photographs if you need care. Never return to an active traffic lane to recreate a view.

Keep any original dashcam files, contact information for witnesses, report numbers, dated photographs and correspondence together. Note when each photograph was taken. A picture of a repaired signal or freshly repainted lane taken a week later may still explain the geometry, but identifying it as a later view keeps it from being mistaken for a record of the crash date. If a business may have a camera, identify its location promptly and ask about retention. Some cameras face entrances rather than the road.

A police report can contain an officer’s observations, statements by the drivers, or conclusions based on the scene after the vehicles moved. Read who supplied the detail at issue. If the report gives the wrong lane, identify the passage and what supports a correction. The [guide to correcting an inaccurate crash report](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) explains how to raise a factual issue with the investigating agency.

## What speed can and cannot explain about an injury claim

Even when a speed was unsafe, the claim still turns on how the collision happened and what harm followed. Imagine a vehicle pulling out of a driveway across a driver’s path. The driveway driver’s view, the approaching driver’s speed and the time each had to react can all matter. Identifying one mistake does not establish how much of the outcome it explains. The [California right-of-way guide](/blogs/right-of-way-accident-claims-california) offers a way to map the paths before assigning responsibility.

Keep treatment, lost-work and repair records with the chronology, while identifying what each one documents. A repair estimate can locate damage. A medical visit records care and reported symptoms. Neither tells you exactly how fast the other car was going. When talking to the [Buena Park car accident practice](/practice-areas/car-accidents), bring the documents you have and identify the question you need help answering, especially if an insurer has asked you to agree to a percentage of fault or sign a release.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a posted 45 mph limit mean 40 mph was safe?

It depends on the approach. Forty miles per hour on a clear, dry road presents a different situation from the same speed approaching stopped traffic beyond a blind bend. Record the sightline, pavement and traffic in the lane involved. Include the posted limit and explain where the sign stood in relation to the crash.

### Can vehicle damage tell me the other driver’s exact speed?

Damage photographs are worth saving, but they do not give a dependable speed reading on their own. The vehicles involved, angles of impact and movement before and after contact all affect what you see. Preserve repair photographs and estimates alongside video and witness information. Avoid turning a visual impression into an exact speed in a claim statement.

### What if the officer did not issue a speeding ticket?

You can still document what made the speed unsafe and ask for a review of the circumstances. An officer who arrived afterward may have seen the final vehicle positions without seeing the approach. Save the report and any independent evidence. The absence of a citation leaves the underlying facts open for investigation.

### What if the only video starts at impact?

Save it anyway. It may establish the time, vehicle positions, lighting or where the cars came to rest. Note that it starts at impact. Ask whether the camera retained an earlier segment and whether nearby businesses or other drivers had a view of the approach. A limited recording is useful when its limits are clear.
