## Start with the paths that crossed

Imagine a driver turning left across two lanes outside a shopping center. A delivery van in the first oncoming lane stops and leaves a gap. The turning driver sees a wave, begins the turn, and is hit by a car in the second lane. Three people can give honest accounts of that collision and still describe different things. The van driver saw a gap. The turning driver saw permission to pass the van. The approaching driver saw a vehicle emerge from behind it. Each person’s view had a boundary.

A useful account starts one step before impact. Which street were you on? Which direction were you facing? Were you in a through lane, a turn pocket or a driveway? Draw the two paths and mark where the vehicles first became visible to one another. If you only saw the other vehicle when it appeared in your side window, say so. Someone reading the file months later needs to know which part of the sequence you witnessed.

## Identify the traffic control for your particular movement

Two drivers can face green lights at the same time and still have different obligations. A driver turning left on a circular green may have to wait for approaching traffic. A green turn arrow conveys different information. A photo of the intersection taken a day later can identify whether an arrow exists. It cannot establish whether the arrow was illuminated at the moment of the crash. Describe the signal you personally saw, including its shape if you remember it.

At an all-way stop, the order in which vehicles reach the intersection matters. When vehicles arrive at the same time, the driver on the left ordinarily yields to the one on the right. At a two-way stop, that shorthand can be misleading. A driver waiting at the stop sign cannot assume traffic on the through street must stop too. Look at the controls facing each approach before relying on a rule remembered from driver training. [California Driver’s Handbook](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/california-driver-handbook-pdf)

A turn signal tells other road users what someone intends to do. It does not establish when the turn began, whether a safe gap existed, or whether the indicator was visible from another lane. If someone says they signaled, record that as their account. If you actually saw the indicator flashing, record when you saw it relative to the movement across your path.

## When a driver turns left or enters from a driveway

For a left turn, the practical question is how close approaching traffic was when the driver began crossing its path. A clear view past the first lane matters. So does whether the approaching vehicle was traveling straight, changing lanes or emerging from behind something larger. Describing the visible landmarks can be more reliable than trying to estimate the other vehicle’s speed. “It came into view beside the bus shelter” gives someone a detail they may be able to check. [DMV guidance on making turns](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/navigating-the-roads/)

A driveway adds another crossing point before the driver even reaches the travel lanes. A vehicle can cross a sidewalk used by people walking or cycling, pause at the street, and then enter traffic. Those are separate movements. If you are reporting a [Buena Park driveway crash](/locations/buena-park), identify the entrance by the business name and the direction you were traveling. A shopping center may have several entrances with different sight lines, and the street address alone can send an investigator to the wrong one.

## What a witness can actually confirm

Ask where the witness was standing and which way they were looking. Someone in the passenger seat may have watched the traffic signal but missed the approaching vehicle. Someone at a bus stop may have seen both vehicles while facing away from the signal. A witness who heard braking and then turned around can describe the sound and the aftermath. Their account is still useful when its limits are clear.

Keep the witness’s contact details and their words if they agree to provide them. Avoid filling gaps by suggesting answers or circulating a group version of events. If a nearby business might have footage, record which exterior wall faced the approach and the approximate time window. A camera pointed at the entrance may show a vehicle’s reflection or its arrival just before the crash even when the collision itself falls outside the frame.

## Preserve the part of the recording before impact

A dashcam clip that begins at the sound of contact may miss the strongest evidence. Preserve the original file and any preceding segment that shows the approach. Save a viewing copy separately. Note if the camera was pointing forward while the other vehicle approached from the side, and whether the time displayed on the recording differs from the actual local time. Send a copy through an agreed channel and keep the original.

Photographs have different jobs. An overview helps locate the intersection. A closer view can show lane arrows, signal placement or an obstruction. Damage photos help identify contact points. Keep track of when each group was taken. A delivery truck photographed the following afternoon illustrates where a truck might obstruct a view, but it does not prove one was parked there during the collision. ## When the report and your account differ

Read the report in parts. Identify statements attributed to drivers, statements attributed to independent witnesses, the officer’s observations, and any conclusion about the cause. If the report says you were traveling west when your route ran east, that is a specific fact you can address. If it accurately describes your route but reaches a conclusion you dispute, explain which evidence bears on that conclusion.

A useful correction request identifies the report number, the passage at issue, and the material that supports your account. Keep a dated copy of the request and any response. A request does not guarantee a change. The original report and your supporting documents may both remain relevant to the insurer. ## If the insurer says both drivers contributed

Ask which actions the insurer attributes to each person. “You had time to avoid the collision” raises questions about when the danger became visible and what room was available. A person traveling through an intersection can have priority and still face questions about speed, lookout or response. Conversely, the fact that someone could describe a possible evasive maneuver afterward does not establish that the driver had time to use it.

California’s comparative fault rules can reduce an injured person’s recovery according to their share of responsibility. An insurer’s proposed percentage is its assessment. Ask for the reasons in writing and distinguish an agreed fact from a disputed inference. If the dispute involves two vehicles already moving in the same direction and one entering the other lane, the [unsafe lane-change guide](/blogs/unsafe-lane-change-accident-fault-california) addresses that different sequence. For a broader explanation, see [California comparative fault](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury).

## Bring a short timeline to the first conversation

Start with a single page. On the first line, identify the intersection and direction of travel. On the next lines, describe the traffic control, the first moment you noticed danger, the impact and what happened afterward. Mark details supplied by another person. If you were hurt and have no memory of the impact, leave that interval open and note whether witnesses, photos or recordings may help reconstruct it.

Keep injury and repair records in separate folders. An estimate may help show where a vehicle was struck. Medical records describe the care you received. Neither document by itself tells you who had priority. If you have an insurer letter, bring the complete letter, including any response date, and explain whether a repair payment or injury release has already been offered. ## Frequently asked questions

### Does the driver who hits the side of a vehicle always have the right-of-way?

No. Side damage helps establish where the vehicles touched. The same damage pattern could follow a left turn across traffic, a vehicle entering against a signal, or a lane change near an intersection. To understand priority, place the damage beside the controls facing each driver, their direction of travel and the sequence before impact. Preserve photographs of both vehicles when available.

### What if another driver waved me through?

Explain exactly who waved, where their vehicle stopped and which lanes they could see. A driver can leave space ahead of their own vehicle without knowing whether an adjacent lane is clear. If a witness saw the wave, ask whether they also saw approaching traffic. The account of the person making the gesture can help explain why a turn began while leaving other questions open.

### What if there was no ticket?

Start with the same evidence you would gather if a ticket had been issued. The officer may have arrived after both drivers moved their vehicles. Identify whether the officer saw the event, interviewed a witness or reviewed footage. The absence of a citation does not settle an injury claim. The physical evidence and accounts of people who saw the approach can still support an investigation.

### What if I remember more after the first call?

Write down the additional detail, the date you remembered it and what prompted it. Distinguish a memory from something you learned by watching a video later. Give the insurer or your attorney a dated clarification and keep a copy. An honest account can include uncertainty. A guess written down as a certainty is much harder to untangle later.
