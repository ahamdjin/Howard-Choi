## Follow the money in the order it moves

First, your insurer decides whether it owes a payment under your policy. It might approve repairs, subtract your deductible, and pay the rest to you or the repair shop. The carrier can then investigate whether it can recover the amount it paid from another responsible party. The payment and the recovery are separate events. The first can take place while the second is still uncertain.

Subrogation is different from opening a claim against the other driver yourself. Your insurer is pursuing the part it paid and, where applicable, your deductible. You may still have unreimbursed property expenses or a separate injury claim. Keep records of each. A carrier that paid for a fender does not necessarily represent you in negotiations about lost wages or ongoing medical care.

The process can involve another insurance company, the person responsible, or a dispute resolution process between insurers. Your insurer may ask you to provide photographs, contact information, or an accurate account of the crash. Read the policy’s cooperation provisions and keep the information you send. If the insurer asks for a document you do not have, tell it how you tried to get it and what you can provide instead.

## When will your deductible be part of the recovery?

California’s Department of Insurance explains that if your insurer pursues subrogation, it includes your deductible in the recovery process, subject to the applicable rules. If it decides against pursuing recovery, ask for that position in writing so you can evaluate any options for seeking your deductible. Ask how a partial recovery will be apportioned and whether any permitted expenses would affect the amount paid to you. [California fair claims regulations](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/130-laws-regs-hearings/05-CCR/fair-claims-regs.cfm)

Suppose an approved collision repair costs $6,000 and your policy has a $750 deductible. Your insurer pays $5,250. In a simplified example with no expenses, a recovery of 60 percent of the combined $6,000 claim would correspond to $450 of your deductible if it is apportioned proportionally. The actual result depends on the recovery and the rules that apply. Ask for the insurer’s written calculation if the amount seems different from what you expected.

The [car insurance deductible guide](/blogs/car-insurance-deductible-after-accident-california) explains the initial amount you may have to pay. Subrogation addresses a later recovery, so the two figures may appear on different statements months apart.

Do not confuse a deductible with an unapproved repair or storage charge. If the shop collected $900 from you and the deductible is $750, ask what the extra $150 represents. The carrier may be pursuing recovery of a deductible without seeking that separate expense. Keep the original invoice and proof of payment. Ask whether the balance is a covered charge, a disputed charge, or an item you need to raise separately.

## Why a claim may take time

One insurer may accept that a collision occurred while disputing how responsibility should be divided. An insured driver may be difficult to contact. A repair charge may be disputed even after fault is agreed. If the at-fault driver’s coverage is insufficient, the amount actually collectible may be limited. Each problem calls for a different kind of follow-up.

When you ask for a status update, find out which question remains unresolved. “In subrogation” could mean the demand has been sent and the carrier is waiting for a response. It could also mean the carrier is collecting evidence or has chosen to dispute the other insurer’s position. Ask when the last step happened and what comes next.

If the dispute is about fault, provide evidence tied to the disputed part of the crash. A photograph of the damaged door may support a point about impact location, while a camera clip may show when a vehicle entered a lane. The [California comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) explains how a fault allocation can affect recovery. Ask how an allocation or compromise would affect your deductible.

## What to do if the other driver offers to pay you directly

Ask for the full written proposal. A payment described as covering a deductible can come with language releasing all claims arising from the crash. It can also interfere with your carrier’s recovery. Talk to the carrier about any policy requirements and check what the release would do to other losses before agreeing.

For example, the other driver offers $750 after your insurer has already paid $5,250 to repair the car. They ask you to sign a release that mentions the crash but does not limit the release to the deductible. Give the document to your insurer and ask whether accepting the payment would affect the repair recovery. If you were hurt, review whether the same wording could close your injury claim.

If you receive any payment, record who paid it, what it covered, and the date. Tell the relevant insurer about it. Reimbursement after a crash is easier to track when the records explain why the money changed hands, not just how much was received.

## Keep the property and injury recovery files separate

Use one file for the insurer’s repair payment, deductible and subrogation updates. Use another for treatment bills, health plan payments, wage loss, and any proposed injury settlement. A health plan or another payer may assert separate reimbursement rights. Ask for an itemized explanation and the documents that support it. An auto insurer’s property recovery does not tell you what another plan can seek from an injury settlement.

Write down any deadlines or requests for action. An insurer’s recovery efforts should not be assumed to preserve every separate claim you might have. Review the [California personal injury deadlines guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-deadlines) and seek a claim-specific review if a deadline or release is unclear. ## How to read the final recovery letter

Start with the claim number and the type of loss. Confirm that the insurer is talking about the same repair, deductible and policy you have in your records. Ask it to show the amount demanded, the amount collected, your deductible and the calculation of your portion. If it deducted expenses, request a breakdown and ask how they were shared. If there was a compromise over fault or the repair bill, ask which part of the recovery it affected.

For example, imagine a letter stating that the claim closed with a $300 payment to you. You paid a $750 deductible and expected it all back. A partial collection might explain the difference, but a specific calculation lets you check. Compare the letter against the carrier’s original estimate, any supplements and the receipt showing what you actually paid. Then request an explanation for any difference that remains.

### If you have a separate settlement offer

An offer from the other driver’s insurer may address costs your own policy did not cover. Ask whether it includes sums already paid by your insurer and whether the release includes the insurer’s recovery rights. Confirm with your own carrier before agreeing. If the offer also includes injury losses, keep the itemized vehicle amounts separate so you can see what is actually being offered for medical bills, lost income or other harm.

If your insurer did not recover the deductible, find out whether it closed the matter entirely or still expects payment later. Ask for its position and retain the closure letter. The other driver’s carrier may ask what was paid and what has already been released. The paperwork gives you a basis for answering those questions accurately.

### When more than one payer wants money back

Medical bills paid by an auto insurer, a health plan, or another benefit can raise different repayment issues. Ask each payer to identify the policy, agreement or rule it relies on and the payments it wants repaid. Compare those payments with the provider’s ledger. A request from a health plan should not be added to a vehicle deductible worksheet. Keep the amounts and their supporting documents together by payer so any overlap is visible before a settlement is distributed.

## Put four questions in your next status request

A short written request can make the next update more useful. Identify the claim number and ask the carrier to answer these questions separately.

- Has a recovery demand been sent, and on what date?
- What total amount does the demand include, and how much of it is my deductible?
- Has any money been collected, and how will my share be calculated?
- If recovery is no longer being pursued, when was that decision made and what written notice is available?

Attach the deductible receipt if the carrier’s figure does not match what you paid. If the shop invoice contains an additional customer charge, identify it separately. A clear request gives the handler a specific discrepancy to investigate instead of asking for a general promise that everything will be recovered.

If a rental expense remains unpaid, explain whether it was included in the demand or remains a separate request. The [rental reimbursement guide](/blogs/rental-car-reimbursement-after-accident-california) shows which approvals and receipts help establish those expenses. A demand for repair costs does not tell you whether rental costs were also presented.

For medical benefits, begin a different inquiry. The [MedPay guide](/blogs/medical-payments-coverage-medpay-california) explains how to compare medical payments with provider balances. If a medical payer asks for reimbursement, send that request for review with its supporting records rather than treating it as another entry in the vehicle recovery calculation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will I get my deductible back automatically?

Recovery is not guaranteed. Ask whether the insurer intends to pursue it, whether your deductible was included, and whether any amount was recovered. If a partial payment comes in, request the calculation.

### Can I contact the other driver myself?

Ask your carrier about its recovery efforts and your obligations before pursuing the same loss independently. Avoid signing a release until you understand the scope and any effect on insurance benefits or other claims.

### Does subrogation settle my bodily injury case?

An insurer recovering a payment it made for vehicle damage is dealing with that payment. Review your injury claim, other benefits, and any release separately.

### What if the insurer closes its recovery file?

Ask for the reason in writing and whether any amount was recovered. Confirm whether the insurer is still asserting a right to payment and what options are open for your deductible or other unpaid losses.

### What if I receive a recovery payment after I have already been reimbursed?

Identify the payment and notify the relevant carrier before assuming you can keep both amounts. Send the earlier payment record and ask which loss the new payment covers. A later check might represent a different expense, an additional recovery or a duplicate. Keep the written explanation with both payment records.
