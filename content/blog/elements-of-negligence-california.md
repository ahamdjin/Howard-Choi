## The four elements and what can prove each one

In a California injury claim, negligence concerns a failure to use legally required care. An injury by itself leaves several questions open. [Civil Code section 1714](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1714.) describes California’s general ordinary-care principle. The [Judicial Council’s civil jury instructions](https://courts.ca.gov/partners/california-jury-instructions/civil-jury-instructions-resource-center/civil-jury-instructions), including CACI Nos. 400, 401, and 430, explain the basic negligence and causation questions juries may be asked to decide. The applicable instruction depends on the facts and the type of claim.

| Element | Question to ask | A useful record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duty of care | What responsibility did the person or business have in this situation? | A record identifying the driver, property controller, or party responsible for the activity |
| Breach | What did that person do or fail to do that fell below reasonable care? | Full video, scene conditions, eyewitness accounts, or inspection records |
| Causation | How did the conduct contribute to the harm? | The sequence of impacts, medical chronology, or evidence showing what happened before the injury |
| Damages | What losses followed, and how can they be supported? | Treatment records, bills, wage records, repair records, or evidence of daily limitations |

A single record can matter to more than one element. The original video may show the driver’s view and the point of impact. The medical chart may document both when symptoms began and what care you needed. Keep the originals and make a small index explaining what each record shows.

## Duty of care starts with who controlled the risk

Drivers generally must use reasonable care under the conditions they face. In a property case, the first question may be which person or organization controlled the area, supervised the activity, or was responsible for inspecting it. The name on a storefront and the party who managed a parking lot can be different. Save the incident report, the address, the exact location, and the name of the business that took the report. A lawyer can use those details to identify the relevant responsibilities.

Suppose someone falls where a store’s entrance meets a shared parking lot. A photo of the hazard helps, but it may not identify who maintained that section. Start with a wide photograph, an address or unit number, and a description of which side of the doorway it was on. Note who responded and whether they said the site was managed by someone else. Keep any emails or written notices about who handles maintenance. This information is more useful than assuming that whoever owns the property handled each part of it.

## Breach is about the conduct and the circumstances

Ask what a reasonably careful person would have done under the conditions and what the available evidence shows the person actually did. In a collision, the useful details may include the lane, sightline, signal phase, traffic speed, rain, or when a driver could first see the hazard. For a fall, they may include how long a condition was present, what made it visible, and what inspection or response took place.

For example, a driver turns left and strikes someone crossing an intersection. A photo of the damage records where the vehicles or people made contact. Video beginning before the turn may also show when the signal changed, where the pedestrian entered, and whether headlights or another vehicle obstructed the driver’s view. Try to save the complete clip, including time before and after impact. Write down what you observed yourself and identify details you learned later from a witness or recording.

A traffic citation may give you a lead about the officer’s observations or a potentially relevant rule. Save the report number and check which details the officer observed, which came from statements, and whether the report includes a sketch or diagram. For a crash where the other driver disputes the sequence of impacts, see the [rear-end collision evidence guide](/blogs/california-rear-end-collision-fault-evidence). When a report contains an error, use the [California crash report correction guide](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) to organize the evidence supporting a correction.

## Causation connects the conduct to the injury

California’s substantial-factor instruction asks whether conduct contributed to the harm in a meaningful way. More than one condition or person can contribute to a crash and the injuries that follow. In a chain collision, the order of impacts, whether the middle car had stopped, and when symptoms began can matter. Preserve photos of the front and rear of each vehicle when available. Tell the provider if there were separate impacts and note which events you remember clearly. [Judicial Council civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 430–431](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf)

Use two timelines when fault and injury are both in dispute. The first covers the seconds or minutes before the injury. The second covers what you noticed afterward, when you sought care, and what changed at work or home. A witness who saw a vehicle move across lanes may help with the first timeline. A dated medical record or a manager’s email about a missed shift may help with the second.

If you had pain or treatment in the same area before the crash, preserve those records. Establish what you could do before the event, which symptoms existed, and what changed afterward. For a practical way to make the comparison, see the [California pre-existing injury guide](/blogs/car-accident-pre-existing-injury-california). A complete baseline makes it possible to discuss aggravation and competing explanations with actual records.

## Damages are the losses you can describe and support

List financial losses separately from changes in your daily life. For each medical charge, keep the date, provider, bills, and related care records. For missed work, collect the schedule, pay records, and any note that explains work restrictions. If you have future treatment or ongoing restrictions, document the provider’s assessment and what remains uncertain. For how this evidence fits into the larger case, see the [California personal injury guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-guide). The [California Courts personal injury overview](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury) explains how evidence fits into proving an injury case.

Concrete examples also help describe effects that do not appear on an invoice. Keep a dated record of which activities became harder, what assistance you needed, and when those limitations changed. A single weekly note saying you stopped driving at night because turning your head was difficult conveys more than a long list of identical entries. Where a repair bill or replacement cost is involved, keep that on a separate line from care and wage loss.

## Put together an evidence file someone else can follow

Make a one-page timeline using the date, source, and event. Include the intersection or property address, the first available photo or video, when you noticed symptoms, and when your schedule changed. Label estimates and any missing dates. Save witness contact information and the time and place they saw the event. A shared folder can hold copies while you retain the original files and their timestamps separately.

Then circle the point the other side disputes. If it is the driver’s path, look for the minutes before the crash from another camera or witness. If it is whether you lost wages, ask payroll for the schedule and pay period that changed. If both sides agree what happened but differ about fault percentages, see the [California comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury). A specific question helps you prioritize the work while records are still readily available.

## How to identify which part of your case needs more evidence

Start with one sentence for each element and write down which record supports it. If the person at fault is uncertain, list the potential parties and the facts showing who controlled the vehicle, property, or activity. A maintenance invoice can identify who serviced equipment. It may take the contract or inspection logs to see who had responsibility for checking it that day.

If you know who was responsible but disagree about what happened, look for evidence close in time to the event. For instance, in a collision involving a driver who turned across traffic, footage may show the signal phase and sequence of movement. A witness may have seen the turn but had no view of the signal. Record where each witness stood and what each person personally observed. This gives you a way to evaluate different accounts without assuming every detail can be corroborated.

If the dispute concerns causation, compare the mechanism of injury with the treatment timeline. Suppose a driver accepts that a collision occurred and the insurer points to an older shoulder injury. Keep pre-crash and post-crash records that show differences in symptoms, range of motion, activities, and treatment, if available. An impact report gives context about the event, while a clinician can assess whether a new diagnosis or worsening of an existing condition fits the medical record.

If the dispute concerns the amount of loss, pair each type of loss with its source. A decision to put you on light duty may appear in a work note, while pay statements can show whether hours or earnings changed. Save both and keep the date ranges aligned. An organized timeline also makes it easier to explain gaps or identify which provider, employer, or witness may still have a record.

## Frequently asked questions about proving negligence

### Is a police report enough to prove negligence?

A report can identify witnesses, describe scene evidence, and record what people said. A claim also needs evidence connecting the conduct to your injury and losses. Ask what the report author observed firsthand and what additional records support the parts the other side disputes.

### Do I need video or an independent witness?

No single kind of proof is required in every case. Vehicle damage, photographs, schedules, inspection records, and statements may help establish what happened. Make a clear note of what each item can establish and which questions remain open.

### What if I may have been partly at fault?

California’s comparative fault rules can reduce recovery by the claimant’s share of responsibility. Ask which specific action the other side says contributed to the injury, and preserve evidence of what both parties could see and do at that moment. [Judicial Council civil jury instructions, CACI No. 405](https://courts.ca.gov/partners/california-jury-instructions/civil-jury-instructions-resource-center/civil-jury-instructions)

### What if I did not notice symptoms until later?

Write down when you first noticed each symptom, when you sought help, and any other events or prior symptoms that could matter. Delayed symptoms make a precise timeline and consistent treatment records useful. Share the actual dates with your provider and attorney so they can evaluate the link.

### Can a rule violation establish negligence by itself?

A safety rule can matter if it applies to the people and harm involved. The evidence still has to address whether it contributed to the injury and what losses resulted. A lawyer can check whether the facts support using California’s negligence-per-se instruction, CACI No. 418. [Judicial Council civil jury instructions](https://courts.ca.gov/partners/california-jury-instructions/civil-jury-instructions-resource-center/civil-jury-instructions)

### What should I bring to the first conversation?

Bring the event date and location, the best records of what happened, your first treatment record or appointment information, and an example of a bill or work change. Include any insurer letter that names a disputed issue or a response date. A short, dated list helps the reviewer see which of the four elements needs more proof.
