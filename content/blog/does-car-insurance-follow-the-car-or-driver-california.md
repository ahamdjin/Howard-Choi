## Start with the car, then ask about the driver

The expression “insurance follows the car” is a starting point for finding policies. It is a poor substitute for reading them. Imagine you lend your car to a friend for a grocery run. On the way back, the friend hits another car. The people in that car may have injury claims, your vehicle may need repairs, and your friend may have medical bills. The same collision has created three different coverage questions.

The policy listing your vehicle is the first one to check for liability coverage for the borrower and physical damage coverage for your car. The borrower’s policy may address use of a car they do not own. It can contain limits, exclusions, and rules about how it coordinates with another policy. If an insurer says its policy is secondary, ask what must happen before that coverage can respond. “Secondary” means little until you know which claim, which other policy, and which conditions the adjuster is talking about.

Pull the declarations page for each policy to check the coverage dates, insured vehicles and limits. Then obtain the actual policy and endorsements. The declarations are a useful index, but a named-driver exclusion or an endorsement about business use may be on a different page. ## What permissive use means in practice

Permissive use is driving with the owner’s permission. Permission might be easy to establish when the owner handed over the keys and texted that the friend could use the car for the afternoon. It can get harder when several household members use the same vehicle, when the borrower hands the keys to someone else, or when the purpose of the trip changes. Tell the insurer what was agreed and what you know firsthand. Save the messages rather than reconstructing the agreement from memory.

A one-time loan and daily use are worth treating separately. If your cousin has been using your car to commute for a month, say so when reporting the claim. The insurer may ask where the vehicle is kept, who regularly drives it, and whether the cousin lives in your household. Ask which policy provision makes that information relevant. You can give an accurate account without accepting the conclusion that the insurer has drawn from it.

If the driver is explicitly excluded, obtain the signed exclusion and check the name, dates, and scope. If the trip was for deliveries or rideshare work, find out what the driver was doing at the exact time of the crash. App status, trip records, and the vehicle’s use may matter. The [Uber and Lyft insurance guide](/blogs/uber-lyft-accident-insurance-california) explains why a few minutes can change which policy an adjuster examines.

## Separate the losses before deciding who should pay

**Damage to the other person’s car.** If your friend caused a crash while using the vehicle with permission, the other driver may pursue a property damage liability claim. Get the insurer’s coverage position, the available limit, and the name of the person assigned to evaluate the damage. If more than one vehicle or piece of property was damaged, ask whether those losses draw from the same limit.

**Damage to your own car.** Your vehicle’s collision coverage may provide a route to repairs if the loss qualifies. Check [how the collision deductible applies](/blogs/car-insurance-deductible-after-accident-california) and ask for a written breakdown of the payment. The other driver’s liability coverage may also come into play if that driver shares responsibility. Be clear about which insurer is inspecting the car and whether either has authorized storage or a rental.

**Injuries to the borrower or passengers.** A person’s relationship to the owner and their status as a driver or passenger can affect the coverage inquiry. Request the names of the available injury coverages and find out whether they have separate claim numbers. Keep medical bills and benefit explanations even if the vehicle repairs have already been approved. If another driver had inadequate insurance, see the [uninsured and underinsured driver guide](/blogs/california-uninsured-driver-hit-and-run-injury-claims).

**A claim against you as owner.** Ownership, permission, and the driver’s conduct are distinct facts. If an injured person names both you and the driver, send the notice to the insurer promptly and ask whom it is defending. If the insurer reserves its rights, ask which issue is still open and obtain the letter. Keep any summons and the date it was received.

## A two-policy example

Say Maya borrows her brother’s sedan while her car is at a shop. On the way home another driver strikes the sedan and disputes who had the green light. Maya has her own policy. Her brother has collision coverage on the sedan and expects to need it before the other insurer completes its investigation.

They can give both insurers the same objective details while keeping their requests separate. The brother can ask his carrier whether it can inspect the sedan and what deductible applies. Maya can ask her carrier which benefits, if any, extend to her while driving the borrowed car. They should preserve the traffic signal location, nearby camera locations, passenger accounts, and messages about permission to use the sedan.

Now change one fact. Suppose Maya uses the sedan for work every weekday. That regular access is a reason to check her policy’s definition of a covered nonowned vehicle and any endorsements on her brother’s policy. It does not change the facts about the traffic light. Keep the evidence of how the crash happened in one part of the file and the evidence of vehicle use in another. That makes it easier to challenge the specific point an insurer disputes.

## What to ask in the first two calls

- Which policy period and vehicle are you reviewing? Can you send the relevant forms and endorsements?
- Are you investigating permission, household use, an exclusion, business use, or the cause of the crash?
- Who is handling repair approval? If the car must be moved, where can it go and who will pay for it?
- If another policy might respond, what information do you need from it, and who will contact that insurer?
- Are vehicle damage and bodily injury being handled by different adjusters? What is each claim number?
- If there is a coverage dispute, can you explain in writing which provision and which facts support your position?

Note the name and department of the person who answers. Our guide to [speaking with an insurance adjuster after a crash](/blogs/what-is-an-insurance-adjuster-car-accident) explains how to handle requests for a statement or supporting records. One insurer may give separate claim numbers to the vehicle owner, driver, and passengers. Keep them next to the corresponding bills so a request about one person does not get sent to the wrong desk.

If the dispute concerns fault, the [California comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) explains why a percentage assigned by an adjuster can be challenged with evidence. ## What changes if the car belongs to an employer or rental company?

A vehicle supplied by an employer adds two questions. Was the driver authorized to use it, and was the trip connected to work? Save the assignment, schedule, messages, and any vehicle checkout records. A short personal stop during a work trip and a completely personal weekend trip can require different inquiries. Ask the company who handles insurance notices and whether it has sent the crash to a commercial carrier. The driver should keep copies rather than assuming the employer has forwarded everything.

With a rental, identify the renter, anyone authorized to drive, and the agreement in effect when the crash occurred. A damage waiver can address a different risk from liability for injuries to others. A credit card benefit may require a particular payment method or apply only to the rented vehicle. Ask each provider for the terms it is reviewing and note any deadlines for notice. Keep the reservation, signed rental agreement and final invoice together. Those records help explain whether an apparent insurance gap is about the person, the vehicle or the kind of loss.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can my uninsured friend drive my insured car?

The absence of the borrower’s own policy does not resolve whether the owner’s policy covers the trip. Check whether the borrower had permission, whether any exclusion applies, and whether their use of the vehicle had become regular. Ask which limits apply to that driver and which losses those limits address.

### Does the borrower have to be listed on the policy?

A person borrowing the car once can present a different question from a person who lives with you and drives it repeatedly. Give the insurer accurate details about where the car is kept and who uses it. Ask whether the policy required that person to be listed and whether an endorsement changes the answer.

### Can I ask my insurer to pay before the other company decides fault?

Ask whether you have applicable collision coverage or another benefit on your own policy. If you proceed under your policy, confirm the deductible and how the insurer will handle any later recovery. Tell both companies about payments already made so each can track the correct loss.

### Does giving someone permission make me responsible for every loss?

Permission is one fact in the ownership and coverage analysis. The driver’s conduct, the cause of the injuries, the nature of the claim against the owner, and the applicable policies still need review. Forward any claim or lawsuit to the insurer and preserve the documents showing who had the car and why.
