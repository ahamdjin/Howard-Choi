## What a gradual work injury can look like

A warehouse employee moves items from waist height to an overhead shelf. At first, their shoulder aches after the busiest shifts and feels better on days off. Months later, they start switching arms halfway through a shift. One morning they need help lifting a carton they used to lift alone. Asked which box caused the injury, they have no honest answer. What they can describe is the change in the job and in their ability to do it.

California recognizes both specific and cumulative injuries. Under Labor Code section 3208.1, cumulative injury can result from repetitive mentally or physically traumatic activities over time whose combined effect causes disability or a need for medical treatment. A single identifiable event can raise a different or additional issue. A clinician evaluates symptoms and possible causes; the worker supplies the history that helps make that evaluation meaningful. [California Labor Code section 3208.1](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=3208.1&lawCode=LAB)

An injury building over time can be difficult to explain in a short intake call. Start with an ordinary shift and the first change you can date with reasonable confidence. You might remember a transfer to a new department because it happened after a holiday. You might remember asking a coworker for help because that person began training the following week. Identify these as reference points. There is no advantage in assigning a precise day to a memory that covers a month.

## Make the job visible to someone who has never done it

A job title rarely captures the physical demands of the work. Two employees listed as machine operators might spend their shifts very differently. One loads material by hand and reaches into a fixture. The other monitors a display and makes occasional adjustments. A doctor who hears only the job title may miss the movement that concerns you. Describe what your hands, arms, back or legs actually did during a typical cycle and what changed by the end of the shift.

Useful details include the height of a work surface, the reach involved, how long a task usually lasted, the weight range if you know it, and whether duties rotated. Explain what varied. A person who normally shared unloading duties may have worked alone while a coworker was out. A cashier might have worked shorter shifts during the school year and longer ones over the summer. Estimate honestly and explain the basis for the estimate. If the weight was printed on a package, say so. If it was a guess, say that instead.

Use records you already possess or can properly request. Schedules, pay stubs, assignment messages or written restrictions can anchor the dates. A training manual may describe how a task was designed; explain if the actual workstation or staffing differed. Respect workplace privacy and restrictions on records. There is no need to take confidential customer information, copy someone else’s medical information or return to a painful activity to produce proof.

## Keep four dates separate

A gradual injury can involve several important dates: the first symptoms, the first change in work capacity, the point when a work connection became apparent, and the date the employer was notified. A fifth date, such as the first medical visit, may help explain one of the others. Keep the dates separate even if the form or person interviewing you initially asks for only one.

California has a particular legal rule for determining the date of injury in cumulative cases. Labor Code section 5412 looks to disability and when the employee knew, or with reasonable diligence should have known, that the disability was caused by their current or earlier employment. Applying that rule can require a review of the work and medical history. The last day on the job, the first ache and the first diagnosis may all be different days. Do not choose a legal date simply because it fits into a form field. If you are unsure, explain the range to the person helping with the claim. [California Labor Code section 5412](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=5412.&lawCode=LAB)

For example, a hotel employee might have mentioned elbow pain to a supervisor in June, switched cleaning assignments in July and first discussed a possible work connection with a clinician in August. Save the June message, July assignment and August visit details. Reporting these events accurately gives the reviewer a way to investigate without treating any one event as the answer to every timing question. Seek individual advice promptly if there is a disagreement about dates or a deadline.

## Report the concern and keep a copy of the claim form

Tell your employer promptly when you believe work may be causing or worsening a condition. Describe the duties involved, symptoms you can identify and how the problem has affected your ability to work. If you reported it verbally, make a dated note of who received the report and what you said. If a supervisor changes or you work through a staffing agency, keep the names of the people and companies you told.

The California Division of Workers’ Compensation explains how to obtain and return the DWC-1 claim form. The employer generally must give or mail the form within one working day after learning about a work injury or illness. Complete the employee portion, keep a copy, and retain a record of delivery. If the employer does not provide the form, the state makes it available directly. Its [instructions for filing a claim](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dwc/FileAClaim.htm) explain the steps. For the broader sequence after reporting, see the [California workers’ compensation filing guide](/blogs/workers-comp-step-by-step-process-of-filing-a-workers-comp-claim-in-california).

If a manager says to see how you feel after a weekend, record the conversation and ask how to obtain the claim form and information about care. A scheduling accommodation may help you get through a shift, but it may leave no usable record of what you reported. Save the completed copy the employer returns to you. Check contact details carefully so a letter from the claims administrator reaches you even if you change jobs or addresses.

## Describe changes in ability at your medical appointment

Bring a brief account of your work and how your symptoms developed. Concrete examples help: trouble gripping a scanner after several hours, needing help pushing a loaded cart, or being unable to turn a handle near the end of a shift. Explain activities outside work too, including earlier injuries, treatment and changes unrelated to work. The clinician needs the whole picture to assess you.

If there was also a sudden incident, give it its own place on the timeline. A worker might have been experiencing soreness for weeks, then felt a sharp pain catching a falling object. Mention both. Giving the whole history leaves room for the medical evaluation to distinguish the effects of repeated work, a single event or another cause. Avoid trying to settle that question yourself before the visit. If a separate accident caused lasting limitations, the [serious injury practice guide](/practice-areas/serious-injuries) explains how to document changes in mobility, daily care and work for a personal injury claim.

Ask for clarification if a work note is hard to apply. A restriction on lifting may be easy to understand when handling boxes but less clear when pushing a loaded rack or repeatedly reaching above shoulder height. Explain the actual task to the clinician and ask how the restriction applies. Keep the note and the date you provided it to the employer. If duties change, describe the new assignment at follow-up rather than assuming the person treating you knows what the employer offered.

## What to keep if your hours, employer or duties change

Save schedules from before and after symptoms affected work. A pay stub may show fewer earnings without explaining the reason. Keep the message offering shorter shifts, the appointment confirmation for a missed morning, or the note assigning you to different duties. If hours fell because business slowed at the same time, include that information. Distinguishing the two changes makes the record more credible and useful.

A person who has worked the same kind of job for several employers can put the jobs in order, listing locations, approximate dates, hours and duties for each. A payroll employer and the business supervising work at the site may have different names. Bring a pay stub or assignment letter so the claims administrator or adviser can identify both. Avoid guessing which employer or insurer is responsible. The sequence is more helpful than an unsupported conclusion about coverage.

When a letter arrives, keep every page and the envelope or delivery email. Make a note of the date received, who sent it, the decision being communicated, and any response date it mentions. If you are unsure whether the letter concerns treatment, benefits or the claim itself, ask the sender for clarification and seek timely assistance. California’s [Information and Assistance Unit](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dwc/ianda.html) assists injured workers with questions about the system.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I report an injury if I cannot identify a particular accident?

Yes. Explain that the problem developed during repeated work and give the period you can reasonably describe. Include an identifiable incident if one also occurred. Ask for help completing a date field when the exact day is uncertain, and keep a copy of what you submitted.

### What if symptoms became noticeable on a day off?

Tell the clinician and employer when you first noticed them, what you had been doing at work, and how symptoms changed during shifts and rest days. A symptom becoming noticeable away from the workplace does not by itself settle its cause. Your medical history and actual job duties may both be relevant.

### Should I include symptoms I had before this job?

Yes. Describe earlier symptoms, treatment and the activities you could do when you started. Then describe what changed during the present work. A comparison such as “I could complete a full shift until my duties changed” is more informative when supported by approximate dates and an accurate description of earlier limitations.

### Where can I get help if my employer disputes the connection?

Keep the written response, claim number and copies of the records you supplied. Ask which part is disputed: the exposure period, a particular job duty, medical causation or another issue. You can contact a DWC Information and Assistance officer or a California workers’ compensation attorney to discuss the response, options and applicable deadlines. Bring the actual letter so the person reviewing it can address the decision you received.
