**You must submit an SR-1 within 10 days if anyone was injured, anyone died, or the accident caused more than $1,000 in damage to any one person’s property.** This requirement applies regardless of fault. Neither a police report nor an insurance claim replaces the DMV report. [California DMV accident-reporting guidance](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/dmv-virtual-office/accident-reporting/)

For immediate safety, evidence, and insurance steps, see [what to do after a car accident in California](/blogs/what-to-do-after-a-car-accident-in-california). If the collision also caused injuries, our [car accident practice page](/practice-areas/car-accidents) explains how injury claims are evaluated.

Knowing which reports you need can help you meet your obligations while arranging medical care, repairs, and any legal assistance.



## Track the three reports separately

Make a row for each contact you need to make, such as police, your insurer, and the DMV. Put the date, report or claim number, and proof of submission on that row. A police report number can help you complete your records, but verify the SR-1 separately and save its confirmation.

## When California Requires an SR-1

An injury, a death, or property damage above the reporting threshold can each trigger the requirement. An injury does not have to be severe. The DMV’s instructions also cover qualifying crashes on private property, so a collision does not have to happen on a public street or highway to require a report. [Official SR-1 form and instructions](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/web/eng_pdf/sr1.pdf)

The damage threshold applies to **any one person’s property**, not simply the combined damage from the crash. Exactly $1,000 is not more than $1,000. Still, visible damage may not tell you what repairs will cost. An injury requires reporting regardless of the repair bill.

**Hypothetical example.** You back into an unoccupied vehicle in a parking lot. No one is hurt, but repairs to that vehicle are estimated at $1,400. The damage exceeds the threshold, and the private-property location does not remove the reporting obligation.

**Another hypothetical example.** A crash causes $600 in vehicle damage, but a passenger injures their wrist. The injury independently requires an SR-1. These examples explain reporting requirements, not who caused the crash or who may receive compensation.

## Who Should You Notify After a Crash?

Address immediate safety needs first. Check for injuries, call 911 when emergency help is needed, and do not put yourself in danger to collect information. Once those needs are addressed, handle police, insurance, and DMV reporting as separate tasks.

### Police or the California Highway Patrol

California generally requires a written report to the California Highway Patrol or the appropriate city police department within 24 hours of an accident involving injury or death. A responding officer’s report can satisfy that requirement under the statute. This is separate from the DMV’s 10-day deadline and property-damage threshold. [California Vehicle Code section 20008](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=20008.&lawCode=VEH)

For a property-damage-only crash, follow applicable stop-and-exchange requirements. Contact local law enforcement when circumstances call for assistance, but do not assume an officer will attend or prepare a collision report after every call.

### Your insurance company

Notify your insurer promptly, even if you believe another driver caused the crash. Review your policy’s notice and cooperation requirements. Give accurate information, keep claim correspondence, and ask the adjuster which documents are needed.

Reporting a crash does not mean every loss is covered. Liability insurance addresses covered claims against you. Collision, medical payments, and uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage serve different purposes. What your insurer pays depends on the coverage, policy limits, deductibles, and circumstances. [California Department of Insurance automobile insurance guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

### The California DMV

Submit the SR-1 within 10 days when a reporting trigger applies. Your insurance agent, broker, or legal representative may submit it for you. If someone agrees to handle the report, ask for confirmation that it was submitted. Opening an insurance claim alone does not complete this step.

## How to Submit an SR-1

### Gather the information requested on the form

Start with the current official SR-1 and its instructions. You will need the accident’s date, time, and location, along with driver identification, driver’s license information, vehicle details, and registered-owner information. Gather the insurance information that applied on the accident date and the requested details about injuries, deaths, and damaged property.

Keep a separate evidence file as well. Photographs, witness contact information, repair estimates, and medical records can matter even when the SR-1 does not ask you to submit them. The form is not a complete record of your losses or evidence.

### Use an official submission method

The DMV offers an online reporting option and a downloadable SR-1. Follow the current submission instructions, including the mailing address if you use the paper form. You do not need a lawyer to submit a straightforward report.

### Review the details and save confirmation

Check names, dates, vehicle information, and insurance details before submitting. Use the policy that covered the vehicle when the crash happened, not a policy purchased afterward. Do not guess at missing information or present an uncertain detail as a confirmed fact.

Save the completed report and any submission confirmation or mailing record. If you later find an error, contact the DMV about correcting it. Updating your insurer does not necessarily update your DMV report.

## Five Practical Ways to Keep Reporting Organized

**Take photographs safely.** Record visible damage, license plates, vehicle positions, and relevant road conditions when you can do so without entering traffic or delaying emergency care.

**Save witness details.** Keep names and contact information when available. Distinguish what a witness told you from what you personally saw.

**Keep one accident file.** Store photographs, insurance information, repair estimates, medical records, and correspondence together. Protect records that contain personal information.

**Track deadlines separately.** Police reporting, DMV reporting, insurance notice, and lawsuit filing involve different requirements. Completing one does not necessarily satisfy another.

**Address uncertainty promptly.** Missing insurance details, unclear repair costs, or disputed injuries can complicate reporting. Seek guidance early rather than letting the deadline pass while waiting for every question to be resolved.

These records may also help with a compensation claim. California Courts recommends gathering evidence and identifying the people or entities that may be responsible for an injury. [California Courts personal-injury guidance](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

## What Happens If You Do Not Report?

Failing to submit a required SR-1 can lead to suspension of your driving privilege. The DMV may also investigate whether you had the required financial responsibility when the accident occurred. Failing to report and lacking proof of coverage are separate issues, with consequences that depend on the facts and applicable procedures. **Submitting an SR-1 does not automatically cause a suspension.**

Read any DMV notice promptly and follow its instructions. Keep proof of insurance that was effective on the accident date. A current insurance card may not show that you had coverage at the relevant time.

If you face suspension, review the specific notice before assuming a general reinstatement process applies.

## Does an SR-1 Determine Fault or Affect Your Driving Record?

An SR-1 reports an accident. It is not a compensation claim or a determination that you caused the crash. You must report a qualifying collision even when another driver appears entirely responsible. A reportable accident may appear in DMV records, but an accident entry is not the same as a finding of fault.

A police officer’s conclusion about responsibility does not necessarily prevent a DMV accident entry. Likewise, an entry alone does not establish how an insurer will assess liability, coverage, or premiums. Those questions require separate review.

## When Legal Advice May Help

Consider speaking with an attorney if injuries are significant, fault is disputed, insurance is unavailable or inadequate, or you receive a suspension notice. Legal advice may also help you identify responsible parties and preserve evidence.

The SR-1 deadline is not your lawsuit deadline. California personal-injury claims generally have a two-year filing period, but government claims, delayed discovery, claims involving minors, and other exceptions require individual review. Property-damage claims can follow a different deadline. Do not calculate a filing deadline from the date you submitted the DMV report. [California Courts filing-deadline guidance](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/statute-limitations)

Reporting a crash and pursuing compensation remain separate processes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to file an SR-1 if the accident was not my fault?

Yes, if the crash meets a reporting trigger. The requirement applies regardless of who caused the accident, and filing does not itself establish fault.

### Does a police report replace the DMV report?

No. A police report, an insurance claim, and an SR-1 serve different purposes. Confirm that the DMV report was submitted even if an officer investigated the crash.

### Must I report exactly $1,000 in property damage?

Not solely for that reason. The damage trigger is **more than $1,000 to any one person’s property**. An injury or death independently requires reporting, regardless of property-damage costs.

### Do parking-lot accidents require an SR-1?

They can. A qualifying private-property crash is not exempt simply because it happened in a parking lot. Check whether anyone was injured or killed, or whether property damage exceeded the threshold.

### Can my insurer or attorney submit the report?

Your insurance agent, broker, or legal representative may submit it. Ask for confirmation and keep a copy rather than assuming that an insurance claim also completed the DMV reporting requirement.

### What should I do if I missed the 10-day deadline?

Act promptly. Contact the DMV about submitting the report and responding to any notice. Do not assume late filing automatically resolves a suspension issue. Consider legal advice if your driving privilege is at risk.
