## At-Fault and No-Fault Insurance Systems Explained

The main distinction concerns how certain accident-related expenses are paid and when an injured person can pursue a liability claim. Neither system guarantees immediate payment or full compensation.

### How an at-fault system works

In an at-fault system, someone seeking compensation from another driver generally must establish that the driver was legally responsible for the collision and resulting harm. The driver’s liability insurer investigates the claim and may negotiate a settlement within applicable coverage limits.

A lawsuit is not automatically required. Claims can be resolved through insurance negotiations, although litigation may become necessary when responsibility, injury severity, or compensation remains disputed. The amount recoverable also depends on evidence, applicable law, insurance coverage, and the defendant’s ability to pay. [California Courts](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

### How a no-fault system differs

In states with no-fault insurance systems, injured people generally turn first to personal injury protection, commonly called PIP, for specified benefits regardless of who caused the crash. Depending on the state and policy, benefits may include medical expenses, some lost income, and certain replacement services.

“No-fault” does not mean drivers can never be sued. Some states restrict injury lawsuits unless a statutory threshold is met, and those thresholds vary. PIP generally does not compensate for pain and suffering. Property damage may also be handled separately from no-fault injury benefits.

California does not use a mandatory PIP-based no-fault system. However, optional California coverages can pay certain expenses without first establishing another driver’s liability. Those benefits do not change California’s underlying fault-based approach. [California Department of Insurance](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

## Which Insurance May Pay After a California Crash?

Start by identifying the coverage involved rather than assuming one insurer will handle every loss.

### The other driver’s liability insurance

California generally requires drivers to maintain financial responsibility, commonly through liability insurance. Since January 1, 2025, standard minimum liability limits have been $30,000 for injury or death to one person, $60,000 for injury or death to multiple people in one accident, and $15,000 for property damage.

These are coverage limits, not guaranteed settlement amounts. They also may be insufficient for a serious collision. Liability coverage generally pays covered claims arising from the insured driver’s responsibility to others, rather than repairing that driver’s own vehicle. [California DMV](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/vehicle-registration/insurance-requirements/)

### Your own insurance coverage

Your policy may provide several potential routes to payment.

- **Collision coverage** may pay for crash-related vehicle damage, subject to a deductible and policy terms, even while fault remains disputed.
- **Medical payments coverage**, often called MedPay, may cover eligible medical expenses regardless of fault, up to the purchased limit.
- **Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage** may help when a responsible driver has no insurance or insufficient coverage, subject to eligibility requirements and policy terms.

After paying a covered loss, your insurer may seek reimbursement from a responsible party through subrogation. Whether a deductible is recovered depends on the circumstances. Review the policy rather than assuming every expense will be reimbursed. [California Department of Insurance](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

## How Do You Prove Fault After a Car Accident?

A negligence claim generally requires proof of four connected elements. Showing that a collision happened is not enough by itself.

### Duty of care

Drivers generally must use reasonable care to avoid harming others. California’s general negligence statute establishes responsibility for injuries caused by a failure to exercise ordinary care, subject to legal exceptions. [California Civil Code section 1714](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1714.&lawCode=CIV)

### Breach of duty

A breach occurs when a driver’s conduct falls below the applicable standard of care. Depending on the circumstances, evidence of distracted driving, unsafe speed, an improper lane change, or failure to yield may support this element.

### Causation

The unsafe conduct must have caused the claimed harm. For example, evidence that a driver was using a phone does not, by itself, establish that the phone use caused the collision or every injury claimed.

### Damages

The claimant must establish actual harm, such as medical expenses, lost earnings, vehicle damage, or pain and suffering. In an ordinary civil negligence case, the burden is generally a preponderance of the evidence, meaning more likely than not. Meeting that burden requires evidence connecting the defendant’s conduct to the losses claimed. [California Courts](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

## Evidence That Can Strengthen a Claim

Useful evidence tells a consistent story about both the collision and its consequences. Preserve what you can without placing yourself or anyone else in danger.

- Photographs of vehicle positions, damage, road conditions, traffic controls, and visible injuries.
- Witness names and contact information.
- Available dashcam, security-camera, or nearby business footage.
- Police report information and insurance details.
- Medical records, treatment recommendations, bills, and receipts.
- Pay records or employer documentation showing missed work.

A police report can help an investigation, but an officer’s assessment does not automatically resolve civil liability. Medical records are important for documenting injuries and treatment, while photographs or video may help explain the collision itself. Different evidence serves different purposes. [California Courts](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

**Hypothetical example.** A driver changes lanes and strikes another vehicle. Photographs document the damage, but dashcam footage shows how the vehicles moved before impact. Treatment records then help establish whether the collision caused the injured driver’s reported symptoms. No single item necessarily proves the entire claim.

## What Happens If You Were Partly at Fault?

California follows pure comparative negligence. An injured person’s proven damages can be reduced by their percentage of responsibility. Being partly responsible does not automatically bar a claim, but comparative fault does not guarantee a payment either. Liability, damages, defenses, and collectability still matter. [Judicial Council of California Civil Jury Instructions](https://www.courts.ca.gov/partners/317.htm)

**Hypothetical example.** Suppose proven damages total $100,000 and the injured driver is assigned 25 percent of the fault. Comparative negligence would reduce the damages attributable to the other party to $75,000, before other applicable limitations or adjustments.

Fault allocation is not necessarily all-or-nothing. Multiple drivers may contribute to a crash, and their insurers may disagree about the percentages. When multiple defendants are involved, California also distinguishes between responsibility for economic and noneconomic damages. [California Civil Code section 1431.2](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1431.2.&lawCode=CIV)

## What Compensation May Be Available?

Compensation depends on the injuries, supporting evidence, and applicable restrictions.

**Economic damages** address measurable financial losses. They may include reasonable medical expenses, lost wages and employment benefits, reduced earning capacity, and property damage. Future losses require support rather than speculation.

**Noneconomic damages** address harms such as physical pain, emotional distress, and loss of enjoyment of life. These losses are different from out-of-pocket expenses and are not automatically valued by multiplying medical bills. California law also limits noneconomic recovery in some circumstances, including certain claims involving uninsured drivers. [California Civil Code section 3333.4](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=3333.4.&lawCode=CIV)

Punitive damages are not routine compensation for an ordinary driving mistake. They require qualifying evidence under a separate legal standard, including clear and convincing evidence of oppression, fraud, or malice. [California Civil Code section 3294](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=3294.&lawCode=CIV)

## Practical Steps After a Collision

Address immediate safety and medical needs first. When safe, exchange information and document the scene. Notify your insurer promptly and review any reporting or cooperation requirements in your policy.

Keep claim correspondence, receipts, repair estimates, and treatment records together. Before signing a settlement release, understand which claims it resolves and whether your medical condition and future expenses are sufficiently understood.

If responsibility is disputed or injuries are significant, legal advice can help identify potential defendants, preserve evidence, and evaluate coverage. A local attorney can also explain whether negotiation or litigation fits the circumstances.

## Filing Deadlines Require Individual Review

California personal injury claims generally have a two-year filing deadline, while property-damage claims generally have a three-year deadline. These are starting points, not universal rules. Claims involving public entities can require an administrative claim much earlier, often within six months. Different rules may apply to minors, delayed discovery, and other circumstances. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is California a no-fault state for car accidents?

No. California generally uses fault-based liability. A responsible driver can be liable for crash-related harm, although your own policy may provide benefits that do not depend on proving another driver’s fault.

### Can I use my own insurance if the other driver caused the crash?

Yes, depending on your coverage. Collision insurance may address vehicle damage, and MedPay may cover eligible medical expenses. Deductibles, limits, exclusions, and reimbursement provisions can affect what you receive.

### Do I have to sue to recover compensation?

Not necessarily. An insurer may settle a supported claim without a lawsuit. Litigation may be needed when negotiations fail, but a lawsuit does not guarantee recovery or eliminate coverage limits.

### Can I recover compensation if I was mostly at fault?

California’s pure comparative negligence system can permit recovery despite substantial shared fault. Proven damages are reduced by your responsibility percentage, and other legal or practical limits may affect payment.

### What if the other driver has no insurance?

Check whether your policy includes uninsured motorist coverage and collision coverage. Each addresses different losses and has its own requirements. The absence of insurance does not eliminate the other driver’s potential legal responsibility.

### How long do I have to file a car accident lawsuit?

The general California deadline for personal injury lawsuits is two years, but exceptions and earlier government-claim requirements may apply. Have the specific deadline reviewed promptly rather than relying on the general rule.
