## What California’s following-distance rule asks

California Vehicle Code section 21703 requires a following distance that is reasonable for the speed, traffic and road conditions. The [California DMV safe-driving handbook](https://qr.dmv.ca.gov/portal/handbook/california-driver-handbook/safe-driving/) teaches a three-second following rule as a way for drivers to gauge space. The legal question after a crash is what distance was reasonable under the circumstances. Wet pavement, a downhill approach, or a queue hidden beyond a bend can change what the trailing driver could see and how much space the driver needed.

In [Pittman v. Boiven](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2d/249/207.html), the California Court of Appeal discussed both the duty to follow at a reasonable distance and the rule for signaling a sudden slowdown when there is an opportunity to give the signal. Use the rules to identify what you need to document. A photo of two bumpers establishes where they touched. It rarely shows how long the cars were in the same lane, how visible the queue was, or when a driver could react.

## If you were stopped at a red light or in traffic

Write down whether you were fully stopped, approximately how long you had been stopped, and why. You might remember the light had just changed to red, several vehicles were waiting ahead, or your car had been stationary long enough to watch a pedestrian finish crossing. Describe what you actually saw. If you can only estimate the time, label it as an estimate.

Check for a rear-facing dashcam, but also save the front-facing file. It may show the signal, traffic ahead, and how long your car was stationary before the impact. A witness in a neighboring lane may have seen the trailing car approach. Record that witness's location. A pedestrian facing the intersection may be able to identify the signal phase without having seen the trailing car at all.

## If someone says a driver stopped suddenly

Ask what happened immediately before the stop. Braking for backed-up traffic, a pedestrian, or an obstacle can tell a different story from a vehicle slowing without an obvious reason. The issue includes whether the following driver had room to react and whether the lead driver could signal the slowdown. Section 22109’s signal requirement considers whether there was an opportunity to warn the driver behind.

If someone alleges that a brake light was out, preserve photos and service records for the rear lights and note which witness saw the lights before the collision. A lamp broken in the impact cannot establish its pre-crash condition from appearance alone. A clear clip from another angle, a prior repair record, or a qualified inspection may help answer the point. If visibility or road conditions were also disputed, see the [California Basic Speed Law guide](/blogs/california-basic-speed-law-driving-too-fast-for-conditions).

## If the car in front had just changed lanes

Identify where the vehicle started its move, whether it was fully in the lane before braking, and how much space was available after it moved over. A rear impact can follow an abrupt lane change even when there is little or no visible damage to either side of the vehicles. Conversely, an allegation that a driver cut in is hard to assess if the only footage starts after both cars were already in the lane.

Save the original recording from before the lane change through the end of the crash. A few seconds showing the destination lane, lane markings and traffic ahead can be more useful than a cropped clip of the contact. If you received a second angle from a witness, keep it in a separate file and record how you got it. For more on how investigators can evaluate a disputed lane change, see the [unsafe lane-change collision guide](/blogs/unsafe-lane-change-accident-fault-california).

## If you were the middle car in a chain reaction

Write separate answers to two questions. Did your car hit the vehicle in front before you felt a hit from behind? Did the rear impact push your car forward into the first vehicle? If you remember two jolts but cannot tell which happened first, record that uncertainty. Include whether you had been stopped or still rolling.

Photograph the front and rear of each vehicle you can document safely. Record the license plates and where the vehicles ended up. Final positions can differ from the positions at first contact because of the impacts or because the drivers moved afterward. Mark any photos taken after the vehicles were moved.

For example, imagine three cars waiting at a light. The center driver says the last car pushed them forward. The front driver recalls two separate hits, and the last driver says the center car had already made contact. None of those accounts settles the sequence on its own. A video showing the middle car’s brake lights and movement, along with accounts from people who saw the approach, could clarify whether there was one chain reaction or two separate contacts. Give the adjuster that sequence question in writing rather than relying on the position of the cars in the report diagram.

## Which evidence should you collect first?

Begin with safety and emergency help. If you can gather information without putting anyone in danger, capture the signals, lane markings and overall scene as well as close-ups of damage. The [California Department of Insurance accident guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/hadaccident.cfm) provides a starting point for scene records and notifying an insurer. After you leave the scene, save the first copy of the files before making edits or sending them through a messaging app.

- **Video:** Identify businesses, buses or vehicles with cameras and note the time range to request. Preserve full dashcam files before the device overwrites them. Keep the front and rear files if both exist.
- **Witnesses:** Note who saw the approach, the impact or only the aftermath. Save contact details and ask witnesses to describe their own observations.
- **Road layout:** Record the direction, lane and nearest intersection or interchange. Include any temporary traffic controls that affected the approach. Photograph a safe viewpoint and note where you stood.
- **Vehicles:** Keep tow and repair records, photographs of both ends, and original estimates. Note where the vehicle is stored and whether inspection is planned.
- **Reports and claims:** Save the investigating agency, report number, each insurer’s claim number and the dates you received any requests.

Some vehicles have an [event data recorder](https://www.nhtsa.gov/research-data/event-data-recorder) that can capture a short window of technical information, such as speed or driver inputs. Whether useful data exists depends on the vehicle and event. The [federal data-recording standard](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-563/section-563.7) lists vehicle-indicated speed and whether the brake was on or off as separate fields. A brake-on reading can help place braking in the sequence. It does not measure how hard the driver pressed or identify which car made contact first. Ask a qualified person what the recorder captured and how the timing compares with the video and physical evidence.

## What should you do when the insurer disputes fault?

Ask what it disputes and which evidence it relied on. A disagreement about whether a car was already stopped calls for different evidence than a disagreement about which impact came first. Keep the adjuster’s explanation, note any requests or deadlines, and send copies where appropriate while retaining your originals.

California’s [civil jury instructions on comparative fault](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf) address situations where more than one person’s negligence contributed to the harm. An allegation that someone braked late or followed too closely does not by itself assign a percentage of fault. An investigator still needs to connect the conduct to what happened. The [comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) explains how those questions affect a claim.

Keep treatment dates, work restrictions, and other loss records separate from the collision evidence. An insurer may need information about the impact and separate information about what injuries or losses followed. If a letter proposes paying for property damage, read which claims the release includes before treating it as a repair-only payment. The [California third-party insurance guide](/blogs/third-party-car-insurance-claim-california) explains what to track when dealing with another driver’s insurer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use the police report to show I was stopped?

Keep the report and look at whose statement supports that detail. The officer may have seen the stopped vehicles afterward, while a driver or witness gave the account of when they stopped. If the report diagram or narrative seems inconsistent with a recording, preserve both and note the specific point that differs. The [guide to correcting a California crash report](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) explains how to organize that request.

### Does the police report also notify the DMV?

California DMV requires a separate SR-1 report within 10 days if someone is injured or killed or property damage exceeds $1,000. A police report or call to your insurer does not take the place of that filing. Keep a copy of the [DMV submission](/blogs/notify-the-california-dmv-about-an-accident) with the report number and insurer claim IDs. [California DMV reporting instructions](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/dmv-virtual-office/accident-reporting/)

### What if the dashcam clock is wrong?

Save the original file anyway. Note the apparent offset and use a separate record to anchor the timing, such as the dispatch call, a pharmacy receipt, or a witness’s message. Keep your explanation separate from the original recording so someone reviewing it can see how you reached your estimate.

### What is the most useful thing to bring if I only have a few records?

Bring a short timeline with the exact location, the movements you remember before the first impact, and the point you want help clarifying. Add the report number, original photos and any insurer letter you already received. The [Buena Park car accident page](/practice-areas/car-accidents) explains how those records fit into an initial case review.

### What if the bumper looks fine but the repair estimate lists hidden damage?

Keep both the first photos and the shop’s later findings. Ask the shop to identify which parts it examined after removing the bumper cover and whether there are dated photos of the parts underneath. An initial photo and a later estimate record different stages of the inspection. If the insurer disagrees with the estimate, ask it to identify the parts or work it disputes so the shop can respond to those items.
