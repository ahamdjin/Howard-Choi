## What makes a car a total loss?

A car may be declared a total loss when the cost and economics of repairing it no longer make sense relative to its pre-crash value. A repairable car can still be totaled. A bumper that looks like it needs cosmetic work can hide structural or electrical damage, and a repair estimate can increase once the shop takes damaged parts off. Ask the insurer for the estimate it used, including supplements, and when it made the decision.

A total-loss classification answers what the insurer will do with the vehicle damage. It does not establish who caused the crash, how seriously anyone was hurt, or how much is owed on a loan. Keep the inspection findings, insurance coverage decision, and lender balance on separate pages. You can then tell which number you need to challenge and who can correct it.

With your own carrier, applicable collision or comprehensive coverage may pay for a qualifying loss, subject to the policy. Another driver’s [property damage liability insurance](/blogs/what-is-auto-liability-insurance-california) may respond if that driver is responsible and has applicable coverage. The [third-party claim guide](/blogs/third-party-car-insurance-claim-california) explains how to compare that route with using your own policy. With a hit-and-run or inadequate insurance, check the options in the [uninsured driver claims guide](/blogs/california-uninsured-driver-hit-and-run-injury-claims). Coverage for injury and coverage for the damaged car are different inquiries.

## How to check the valuation line by line

The California Department of Insurance explains that an insurer’s cash settlement for a total loss is generally based on a comparable automobile, with the applicable terms and deductions. Ask for the written valuation and the vehicles used in the comparison. You need the details behind the offer to tell whether the insurer valued the right car. [California Department of Insurance’s accident guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/hadaccident.cfm)

**Start with the vehicle description.** Check the VIN, year, trim, drivetrain, mileage, equipment and condition. A mismatched trim can make the estimate look plausible even when the comparison set is wrong. If your vehicle had optional equipment that was missed, supply the window sticker, build sheet, or dated photos. A receipt for recent maintenance can support a correction to condition, though the cost of that maintenance does not automatically add dollar for dollar to market value.

**Check the comparables.** Look for similar vehicles in your market and note differences in year, mileage, options, title history and condition. Save the listing date, asking price, dealer and VIN when available. An expensive listing hundreds of miles away is weaker evidence than a local vehicle with nearly identical equipment. If a comparable is no longer available, say when you checked and save what you were able to verify.

**Read the adjustments and the payment breakdown.** Ask why each adjustment was applied and how it was calculated. Request an itemized explanation of applicable sales tax, transfer or other fees, any deductible and any retained salvage. If you are comparing offers from different carriers, use the same assumptions about ownership of the salvage and the same tax and fee treatment. California’s auto claims standards provide the framework for evaluating comparable vehicles and payment. [California fair claims regulations](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/130-laws-regs-hearings/05-CCR/fair-claims-regs.cfm)

## One example with three different amounts

Suppose an insurer values a car at $18,000 before any taxes and fees. The owner uses collision coverage with a $750 deductible. Ignoring taxes and fees for this example, the resulting payment is $17,250. The bank’s written payoff is $19,000. That leaves a $1,750 shortfall before any other adjustments or benefits.

Each amount has a different job. The $18,000 is a valuation. The $17,250 is the amount calculated under the collision policy in the example. The $19,000 is what the lender says is needed to satisfy the loan on a particular date. If the insurer missed a trim package, provide evidence for a valuation correction. If the loan payoff changed because another payment posted, ask the lender for an updated statement. If there is a GAP agreement, check which part of the balance qualifies and whether it addresses the deductible. Our [GAP insurance guide](/blogs/insurance-how-does-gap-insurance-work) walks through that separate calculation.

The [car insurance deductible guide](/blogs/car-insurance-deductible-after-accident-california) explains how an applicable deductible affects the net payment. A payment can be issued to the owner and a lienholder. Ask who will receive the check and how the balance will be handled. Keep making required payments unless the lender confirms another arrangement. The vehicle’s loss does not by itself close the loan.

## What happens to the car, the rental, and the title?

Before authorizing a transfer, take personal belongings out safely, make copies of available records, and confirm where the vehicle will be moved. Ask who is paying tow or storage charges and on what date that arrangement ends. If you want to retain the car, ask what salvage amount will be deducted and which DMV steps will apply. Keeping a damaged car can affect the payment and introduce title, inspection, repair, and future insurance issues.

Ask the insurer to confirm the rental return date in writing. Coverage can end under the policy after payment or after a time or dollar limit is reached. If you are disputing the valuation, ask how the dispute affects rental arrangements before assuming an extension. A written request lets you make a transportation decision with a known deadline.

If a vehicle is repaired instead, the questions change. A repair estimate addresses what it takes to restore the car. A [diminished value claim](/blogs/diminished-value-claim-california) concerns a possible remaining loss in market value after repairs. A total loss is generally evaluated using the vehicle’s pre-loss value rather than a hypothetical resale loss on a repaired car.

## How to raise a specific dispute

Send [the claims adjuster](/blogs/what-is-an-insurance-adjuster-car-accident) one written request with the valuation, the line you dispute, and your evidence. For example, explain that the report used the base model and attach the VIN details and a comparable with the correct trim. Ask for a corrected calculation, the reasons for any retained adjustments, and an updated settlement offer if the insurer agrees.

If your own policy includes appraisal, ask when it applies, who pays the appraisers, and what it can resolve. A dispute about the amount of a loss can differ from a dispute about whether coverage exists. The California Department of Insurance also describes an [auto claims mediation program](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/AutoMediation.cfm) with eligibility requirements. Check those requirements before treating mediation as an option in your case.

Keep the property paperwork and any injury paperwork separate. If a release is required, read its scope before signing. ## How to make a comparable-vehicle packet

Choose a few well-matched vehicles rather than sending every listing you find. Write the insured car’s model, trim, odometer reading, drivetrain and major options at the top. Under it, enter the same information for each comparable, plus the asking price, listing date and distance from where the car was normally kept. Attach screenshots or copies of the listings because prices and inventory can change while the insurer reviews the claim.

Identify what each listing proves. A local car with the same trim but higher mileage may help show that the trim is available and that the insurer should explain its mileage adjustment. A car with exactly the same mileage but a rebuilt title may be a poor match for a vehicle with a clean title. If none is a close match, say which differences matter and ask how the carrier accounts for them. A simple comparison gives the adjuster specific points to check.

Keep a dated record of every revised report. A corrected description does not necessarily mean the underlying comparables were updated. Compare the old and new figures for tax, fees, deductible and salvage as well as the valuation. If the net offer changed by less than you expected, the payment breakdown should show which other line moved.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I challenge the insurer’s first total-loss offer?

Yes. Start by getting the written valuation. Identify a specific error, such as mileage, trim or a comparison with a materially different vehicle. Send supporting records and ask the adjuster to show the corrected calculation.

### What if I owe more than the car is worth?

Get a current payoff statement and compare it with the insurer’s actual payment. The lender may still require payment of the remaining balance. A GAP agreement may address some of that difference, depending on the contract and any exclusions.

### Can I keep a car that the insurer totals?

Ask whether owner-retained salvage is an option and what it would do to the payment. Confirm the title and registration process with the DMV. Consider the cost of making the vehicle safe and whether you can insure it after repair.

### Does a total-loss payment settle my injury claim?

It depends on the agreement and the scope of any release. Identify which losses the payment covers and which claims the paperwork would close. Ask for a copy of the proposed release before signing or endorsing a settlement payment with conditions attached.
