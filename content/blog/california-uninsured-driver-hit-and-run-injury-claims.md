## Start at the scene: safety and identifying facts

If anyone is injured, get appropriate medical help first and contact emergency services when necessary. Move to a safe place if possible without creating further danger. A departing vehicle can leave only a few seconds of identifying information, but chasing it can create a second crash and should not be attempted.

If safe, record a partial license plate, vehicle color, make or body style, damage, direction of travel, distinguishing features and the approximate time. Write down the location, lane, traffic control, other nearby cars, pedestrians, witnesses and anything the other driver said before leaving. A partial plate and a reliable route description may be more useful than a speculative guess at the driver.

Photograph your vehicle, the surrounding roadway, debris and any visible injury. The California Department of Insurance advises drivers to collect accident details, take photographs and contact the appropriate insurer. Do not delay treatment merely to make a complete record.

## A hit-and-run and an uninsured motorist are not identical

A hit-and-run means a driver left without remaining to provide or exchange the required information; it does not prove the fleeing vehicle was uninsured. An identified driver may have liability insurance, or the vehicle may belong to another insured owner or business. Conversely, a driver who stays may be uninsured or carry limits too low to cover the supported loss.

That distinction matters because different insurance questions follow. A conventional liability claim may still be possible when an at-fault insured party is identified. Uninsured motorist coverage may be relevant when the applicable conditions are satisfied; underinsured motorist coverage addresses the possibility that another driver's liability limits are inadequate.

Write the facts neutrally in the first report. State which details you observed and which you inferred. Do not label someone uninsured simply because they refused to provide an insurance card at the scene.

## What California uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage does

The California Department of Insurance explains that uninsured motorist coverage can pay specified injury losses for covered people after an accident caused by an at-fault uninsured driver. Underinsured motorist coverage can apply in limited circumstances when the responsible driver's liability limits do not adequately cover an injury loss. The state requires auto insurers to offer UM coverage, but a consumer may reject it through the applicable waiver process.

These coverages are contractual and statutory; they are not a promise that any accident or every injured person will be paid. Identify the policy, the person insured, the covered vehicles, relevant endorsements, limits, exclusions, other available liability coverage and any required procedures. Whether a passenger, household member, pedestrian or other claimant qualifies may depend on the exact facts and policy terms.

Ask for the policy declarations and relevant coverage provisions, not just a verbal description that the policy is 'full coverage.' That phrase is too vague to answer whether UM or UIM benefits are actually available.

## Why hit-and-run UM claims deserve separate attention

An unidentified vehicle can make proof and coverage requirements more complicated. Some claims require particular evidence of a motor vehicle's involvement, prompt police reporting or specified forms of notice under applicable law or policy. The legal rules surrounding unidentified vehicles are technical, so a person should avoid relying on a generic blog claim that any non-contact near miss, dashcam clip or single witness automatically qualifies for UM.

Preserve proof of contact where it exists: original vehicle-damage photos, paint transfer, debris, repair estimates, the responding officer's observations and contemporaneous witness accounts. If there was no physical contact, describe that plainly instead of altering the account to fit a perceived insurance requirement. The policy and California law should be reviewed by counsel for the precise incident.

Notify your insurer promptly and keep a dated copy of communications. Ask the insurer to identify relevant notice and documentation requirements in writing.

## Police reports, DMV reporting and time-sensitive video

Report the collision through the appropriate law-enforcement channel and retain a report or incident number. Which agency took the report matters when you later ask for a copy. Video may exist at a nearby store, apartment building, fuel station, bus or business entrance, but many systems overwrite footage on their own schedule.

Record each potential source with exact business name and approximate camera viewpoint and time. Ask about preserving footage; do not assume it will be held indefinitely. Requesting footage can be different from obtaining a copy, and a formal preservation or disclosure process may be necessary.

Separately, the California DMV explains that a driver involved in a reportable California accident must submit an SR-1 within ten days when someone was injured or killed or when the relevant property damage threshold was exceeded. A police or insurance report does not substitute for an SR-1 where the rule applies. Whether you personally must file depends on your involvement and circumstances.

## When the other driver is identified but carries too little insurance

A small liability limit is not the same as no liability policy. An injury that requires surgery, long-term care or lost work may create losses beyond the other driver's available coverage. Underinsured motorist coverage may then be relevant, but the policy's provisions and the handling of the underlying liability claim can affect eligibility.

Before signing a settlement release or accepting the other driver's policy limit, have the applicable UIM notice, consent, exhaustion and other procedural questions reviewed. Resolving one insurance layer may affect another, and a decision that sounds administratively simple could have lasting consequences.

Do not calculate likely UIM recovery by simply subtracting an offer from an imagined case value. Liability, damages, actual limits, offsets, policy language and legal requirements all matter.

## Document injuries independently of the insurance dispute

Insurance coverage describes possible payment sources; medical evidence describes the harm. Keep treatment records, appointment dates, referrals, prescriptions, medical bills and physician-documented work restrictions. A delayed symptom should be reported accurately to a clinician rather than entered retroactively as if it had been observed on the crash date.

Record reasonable out-of-pocket costs, missed work, reduced hours and disruptions to ordinary tasks when they can be tied to the injury. Medical payments coverage or health insurance may cover some immediate costs under their terms, but reimbursement obligations or liens can arise and require separate evaluation.

California Courts explains that injury claims may involve medical bills, lost wages, future treatment, emotional harm and other losses. Not every category is available in every case, and medical bills alone do not establish fault or the amount recoverable.

## How fault disputes complicate UM and liability claims

Before an insurer considers paying injury benefits, the accident may still require proof that another driver was legally responsible. Traffic signals, lane markings, positions of vehicles, witness accounts, camera footage and vehicle damage can determine whether there was a negligent act and what contributed to the collision.

California comparative responsibility can reduce damages where more than one party was at fault. A missing driver may be particularly difficult to investigate, and the involved insurance companies may draw different conclusions from early statements. Preserve the original record so any later explanation can be tested against independent evidence.

Insurer statements are not court findings. Conversely, a police report can be important without being a complete legal determination. Ask counsel what facts remain unproven and what additional evidence can still be obtained.

## Do not overlook owners, employers and other possible defendants

If the driver is identified, the vehicle owner, employment relationship or a business policy might need consideration in some fact patterns. For a delivery or commercial vehicle, photographs of company markings, vehicle identifiers and dispatch or employer information can become significant. For rideshare cases, app status at the time of the crash may change coverage questions.

Investigate each possible party based on the evidence. A vehicle owner or employer is not automatically responsible in every case, and insurance may not be available merely because the business has a recognizable logo.

When the driver left without identifying information, an early inquiry into nearby video, witnesses and vehicle fragments can narrow the possibilities. Counsel can advise whether preservation requests or formal discovery may be available and appropriate.

## Understand the different deadlines

California Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 generally establishes a two-year limitations period for many personal-injury lawsuits, but exceptions and different procedures can apply. Government-vehicle or roadway cases may also trigger earlier government-claim presentment requirements; Government Code section 911.2 generally provides six months for injury-related claims against public entities, subject to facts and exceptions.

These legal filing periods are not the same as an insurance policy's notice, proof-of-claim or UM/UIM arbitration requirements. The correct deadline can depend on coverage documents and the type of dispute. Do not assume negotiating with an insurer suspends a limitations period.

The practical time limit for obtaining surveillance footage or witness information may be far shorter than either. Checking applicable deadlines early is especially important when a defendant is unidentified.

## A usable claim file: what to collect in one place

Keep a folder with the police or incident number; traffic-scene photos; witness contacts; partial vehicle identification; repair estimates; original dashcam footage; insurance declarations, letters and claim numbers; SR-1 confirmation when required; and a medical timeline. Use filenames that include dates so the sequence can be audited.

Maintain separate notes showing which facts you personally observed and which came from a report, insurance representative or witness. This distinction protects the accuracy of the account and may help resolve apparently conflicting information. Keep originals unaltered and back them up securely.

A concise incident summary for an attorney should answer: who was involved, where did it occur, how did the vehicle leave or how was lack of insurance discovered, who investigated, which policies may exist, and what treatment and losses have followed.

## Questions worth asking before agreeing to a settlement

Ask whether the other vehicle and any responsible owner or employer have been adequately identified; which coverage exists; whether UM or UIM coverage applies; what reports and notices the insurer requires; whether important video has been preserved; and whether a proposed release affects other claims. Request clear explanations of deductibles, possible liens and case costs.

If an adjuster asks for a recorded statement or broad medical authorization, ask what it covers and whether it is required under the policy or needed for the specific claim. The answer may differ when speaking with your own insurer versus someone else's company. Avoid blanket rules: your contractual obligations can matter.
