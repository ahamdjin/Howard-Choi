## When to call 911 after a collision

Call 911 for a suspected life-threatening injury. Examples include trouble breathing, severe bleeding that will not stop, loss of consciousness, or new weakness or difficulty speaking. After a blow or jolt to the head, danger signs can include a worsening headache that does not go away, repeated vomiting, a seizure, increasing confusion, or difficulty waking the person. Do not drive yourself for emergency care if you may be seriously injured. Follow the dispatcher’s instructions. [CDC concussion danger signs](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/signs-symptoms/)

A person who is awake, talking and walking may still need prompt medical assessment. A damaged bumper gives little information about what happened to the person inside the car. Likewise, getting out of the vehicle unaided does not answer whether an injury needs treatment. If you are unsure whether symptoms are an emergency, describe them to a qualified medical professional or emergency dispatcher. Do not wait to obtain the other driver’s insurance information before seeking emergency help.

| Situation | Where to turn | What to communicate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A possible emergency at the scene or afterward | 911 or emergency care | Location, current symptoms and changes in alertness or breathing |
| A new concern needing prompt attention, without apparent emergency signs | Your clinician’s triage line or an urgent care service for direction | When the crash happened, what changed and what the facility can evaluate |
| Follow-up after an emergency visit | The clinician or service identified in your discharge instructions | Diagnosis if given, tests performed, restrictions and the requested follow-up interval |
| Persistent symptoms affecting normal activities | The clinician managing your recovery | Which activities are difficult, what has improved and what concerns remain |

## Emergency department, urgent care or your regular clinician?

An emergency department is equipped to assess possible serious injuries and conditions that may require hospital resources. Urgent care centers generally handle less serious concerns when timely care is needed and a regular clinician is unavailable. Their capabilities vary. One center may have basic X-ray services while another may refer a trauma patient elsewhere. Ask before traveling if you have been advised to obtain a particular kind of assessment. For a suspected emergency, go through emergency services instead of calling a series of clinics. [MedlinePlus guidance on when to use emergency care](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000593.htm)

Your regular clinician can be particularly useful after an initial evaluation. They may already know your medications, previous surgeries or symptoms that existed before the collision. That context can help them evaluate a change and coordinate referrals. Tell the office the appointment concerns a recent crash and explain what is happening today. A routine appointment request that says only “annual visit” may not reach the person who handles medical triage.

Consider two people in the same collision. One develops trouble speaking and confusion; the other has already been evaluated, is stable and was instructed to arrange follow-up in a few days. The fact that they shared a car does not mean they need the same destination. The first needs emergency help. The second needs to follow the specific discharge plan and seek reassessment sooner if symptoms worsen. A lawyer, adjuster or online symptom list cannot examine either person.

## What to tell the clinician at the first visit

Start with what you experienced. Say where you sat, whether you were wearing a seat belt, which part of your body struck something if you know, and whether you remember the full event. If you do not know whether your head hit the interior, describe what you do remember. A statement such as “I remember hearing the glass and then being outside the car” is more informative than filling in a missing moment with a guess.

Describe the timing of symptoms in plain language. You might have noticed wrist pain at once but first experienced dizziness when you stood up later. Identify what you were doing and whether the symptom is improving, worsening or coming and going. List current medication, including blood thinners, allergies and prior relevant health problems. If someone with you noticed a change in behavior, they can describe what they personally observed.

It is reasonable to mention an earlier injury. For example, a person may have had intermittent neck discomfort but could drive to work and turn to check a blind spot without difficulty before the collision. Describing the earlier baseline and the new limitation helps the clinician understand the change. It also creates a more accurate history for any later review. ## Leave the first visit knowing the next step

Before you leave, look at the written instructions with a member of the care team if possible. Find out whom to call, when follow-up should occur, whether any results are still pending and what changes should prompt urgent reassessment. If the instructions use a term you do not understand, ask what it means for you at home. A discharge after testing does not necessarily mean every symptom has resolved.

Work and transportation deserve specific questions. Someone who drives a forklift, carries patients or spends long hours at a computer may need different advice about returning to work. Describe the actual job. Ask whether there are restrictions on driving or other activities, how long they apply and who should reassess them. Keep a copy of any work note and follow the clinician’s directions. Do not try to devise your own return-to-work schedule from another person’s experience.

If the office refers you to a specialist or therapy service, ask who will initiate the referral. There is a practical difference between “the office has sent your records and the specialist will call” and “you must contact your health plan for an appointment.” Write down the receiving office, what needs to be sent and when to follow up if you hear nothing. A referral worth having on paper is also worth tracking until you know the next step.

## What if symptoms start after you get home?

Some symptoms following a concussion can appear hours or days after an injury. Follow the instructions you were given and seek medical advice about new or persistent symptoms. Emergency danger signs require emergency care even if an earlier visit was reassuring. The [CDC’s recovery guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/response/index.html) explains follow-up and why instructions about returning to activities should be tailored to the person.

A brief timeline can make a follow-up call more useful. If you are keeping records after a head injury, the [guide to documenting a traumatic brain injury](/blogs/traumatic-brain-injury-evidence-after-california-accident) explains how to organize observations and clinical records. Record when you first noticed the change, what you were doing and whether you are able to eat, sleep, move or concentrate as usual. If a family member is helping, distinguish their observation from your own experience. “My spouse noticed I asked the same question twice” gives a clinician different information from “I felt foggy after lunch.” Bring medication instructions and discharge papers to the follow-up visit if you have them.

## Keep the medical record and the billing record together

Save the after-visit summary, referral, work note and follow-up instructions in one place. Separately, keep itemized bills, health-plan explanations of benefits and receipts. A visit to one building can generate separate bills from a facility and individual clinicians. When a statement arrives, match it to the date and provider before assuming it is new or has already been paid.

If a bill or an in-network question is making it hard to arrange follow-up, ask the billing office or health plan what options are available. Tell the treating office about practical barriers such as transport, work or scheduling instead of quietly missing the appointment. For a suspected emergency, money and paperwork should not delay the call for help. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need the insurer’s permission to go to the emergency department?

If you suspect an emergency, call 911 or seek emergency care immediately. Questions about coverage, billing and the other driver’s responsibility can be addressed separately. Keep the information the provider gives you, including whom to call about insurance or financial assistance once the immediate medical issue has been addressed.

### Do I have to see a doctor recommended by a lawyer?

Choose care based on your medical needs, qualified clinical advice and the options available to you. If someone suggests a provider, ask about credentials, cost, health-plan participation and any documents you are being asked to sign. A recommendation does not guarantee that a particular treatment is necessary or that an insurer will pay for it.

### What if I do not have a primary care doctor?

For an emergency, use emergency services. For a non-emergency follow-up, ask the facility that evaluated you whom it recommends contacting and by when. A health plan’s member services line or a community clinic may help you locate an available clinician. Explain any referral or time frame stated in your discharge instructions.

### Should I request copies of imaging or just keep the bill?

A bill shows that a service was charged. A radiology report records an interpretation; the images are a separate part of the medical record. Ask your clinician what the next provider needs and how the records can be transferred. Keep the contact details for the facility that performed the study, especially if it was separate from your regular office.

### What should I keep for an injury consultation?

Bring the medical timeline, provider names, outstanding bills, written restrictions and insurance correspondence you already have. You can identify missing items without delaying medical care to collect them. If someone disputes what happened in the collision, keep the scene evidence separately.
