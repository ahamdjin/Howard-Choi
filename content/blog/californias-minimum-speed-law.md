## Reconstruct the stretch of road before the stop

If you received a citation, mark where you slowed, what you could see, and where a safe turnout was available. Record whether the traffic behind you had room to pass and how long the conditions lasted. A snapshot of your speed alone leaves out the reason you slowed and the options you had at the time.

## What California’s minimum speed law requires

California Vehicle Code section 22400 addresses driving so slowly that it impedes or blocks the normal and reasonable movement of traffic. Exceptions apply when reduced speed is necessary for safe operation or compliance with the law. The linked statutory reference should be checked against the current text when reviewing a particular citation. [Statutory reference — California Vehicle Code section 22400](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=22400.&lawCode=VEH)

A speed reading alone does not establish the full circumstances. Was traffic actually being obstructed? Was the road clear? Did poor visibility, standing water, damaged pavement, or another hazard make slowing necessary? These questions help distinguish unnecessary obstruction from a reasonable safety decision.

**Hypothetical example.** A driver slows substantially on a clear freeway to look at the scenery while vehicles collect behind them. Another driver travels at the same speed because standing water makes vehicle control difficult. Their speeds are identical, but the reasons for slowing are not.

### How the Basic Speed Law applies

California’s Basic Speed Law, Vehicle Code section 22350, requires a speed that is reasonable and prudent for conditions. A posted maximum does not mean that speed is always safe. Rain, pedestrians, poor visibility, road damage, and traffic may require slower driving. The minimum-speed rule does not override that responsibility.

Do not accelerate into unsafe conditions because someone is following closely. Faster traffic also does not give you permission to exceed the speed limit. Aim for lawful, predictable driving rather than matching the fastest vehicle nearby.

## When slower driving may be appropriate

The same speed may be reasonable in one setting and hazardous in another. The reason for slowing, the surrounding traffic, and the available alternatives all matter.

### Unfamiliar roads and missed turns

Searching for an address or navigating an unfamiliar interchange can draw attention away from traffic. Repeated slowing or abrupt braking for a missed exit can create avoidable conflicts.

Set navigation before leaving and review difficult junctions while parked. If you miss a turn, continue to a safe place to reroute. On winding roads, unfamiliarity may also involve genuine visibility or handling concerns. Do not take a blind curve too quickly just to avoid delaying another driver.

### Rain, fog, snow, and limited visibility

Bad weather can make reduced speed necessary. Fog shortens the distance available to see stopped vehicles. Rain and snow can reduce traction, while poor visibility can make lane markings difficult to follow. Adjust both speed and following distance to the conditions.

**Hypothetical example.** A driver enters dense Central Valley fog and slows because much less roadway is visible. Vehicles gathering behind the car do not, by themselves, establish that the slowdown was unnecessary. The assessment depends on visibility and safe operation, not another motorist’s preferred speed.

### Mechanical problems

A damaged tire, overheating engine, or sudden loss of power may prevent a vehicle from maintaining speed. Focus first on controlling the vehicle and reaching a safe location when possible.

Signal your intentions, use hazard lights when appropriate, and seek assistance. Avoid stopping unnecessarily in an active lane when a safer location is accessible. At the same time, do not attempt highway speeds in a vehicle that cannot operate safely. Hazard lights do not make continued hazardous travel safe.

### Congestion and stop-and-go traffic

Drivers cannot maintain an arbitrary minimum speed when traffic ahead is stopped or crawling. Leave a safe following distance and respond smoothly as traffic changes.

The distinction is between slowing because traffic requires it and unnecessarily blocking an otherwise moving roadway. Accelerating to close every gap, then braking hard, gives nearby drivers less time to respond.

### Nervous or inexperienced drivers

New drivers may find fast-moving roads uncomfortable. That concern deserves attention, but traveling far below a reasonable speed on a clear freeway can create merging and passing difficulties.

Practice with a qualified instructor and choose less demanding routes while building skills. Avoid unnecessary lane changes and use the right-hand lane where appropriate. Confidence should come from better observation, spacing, and control, not from driving faster than conditions permit.

### Construction zones

Narrow lanes, workers, uneven pavement, barriers, and temporary traffic patterns may justify driving below the posted maximum.

Follow signs and traffic-control directions. Allow room for merging and avoid abrupt reactions to cones or equipment. A work zone does not automatically establish a minimum-speed violation. The question remains whether the particular slowdown was necessary under the circumstances.

## Other traffic rules still apply

Speed decisions also depend on posted limits, intersection controls, school-area requirements, railroad signals, and lane-use rules.

A school zone does not automatically require a complete stop simply because it is a school zone. An applicable sign, signal, crossing instruction, or other legal requirement may require stopping. Railroad crossings likewise call for attention to the controls and circumstances, rather than one assumption about every crossing.

If another driver becomes aggressive, avoid brake-checking, racing, or changing lanes unsafely. Allow the vehicle to pass when safe and lawful. Another person’s impatience does not change your duty to drive safely.

## What to check after a minimum-speed citation

Review the specific charge, the evidence, and the court’s instructions. Start with the cited code section, location, alleged conduct, and response deadline. Do not assume every minimum-speed citation carries the same penalties or resolution options.

Confirm potential fines, driving-record consequences, traffic-school eligibility, and insurance effects individually. A single citation should not be described as automatically causing license suspension.

A speed measurement may help show how fast the vehicle was traveling. It does not necessarily explain why the driver slowed or whether traffic was unnecessarily impeded. Preserve evidence of the surrounding conditions as well as the speed.


## Evidence that may support a defense

A useful defense starts with facts, not a promise of dismissal. Consider whether safe operation required the lower speed, whether traffic was actually impeded, and whether the officer’s account accurately reflects the conditions.

Save original dashcam footage before it is overwritten. Write down the time, location, lane, visibility, and traffic conditions while they are fresh. Keep the citation and court correspondence together, along with safely taken photographs and witness contact details. Repair invoices, towing records, and relevant weather or construction information may also help.

Each item should address a disputed issue. A regional weather report may provide background but may not show visibility at the precise location. A repair invoice may document a defect without establishing exactly how the vehicle was driven.

**Hypothetical example.** After a sudden loss of engine power, a cited driver saves dashcam footage and a same-day towing record. Those materials may help explain the slowdown more clearly than a general statement that the car was having trouble. They do not guarantee dismissal.

## When slow driving is involved in a collision

A traffic citation and a personal injury claim address different questions. The citation concerns an alleged violation. An injury claim examines responsibility for the collision, causation, and legally recoverable losses. Identifying the appropriate defendants and preserving evidence are important early steps. [California Courts personal injury guidance](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

The slowest driver is not automatically responsible. Following distance, attention, braking, speed, and lane changes may all matter. California’s comparative-fault principles can reduce damages based on a person’s share of responsibility. They do not guarantee recovery.

Medical expenses and lost earnings are economic losses. Pain and suffering are generally noneconomic losses. Punitive damages require qualifying evidence and are not routine compensation for a crash. The available claims and damages depend on the evidence and applicable law.

Insurance coverage is a separate issue. Liability, collision, uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, and medical payments coverage serve different purposes. A policy may not cover every loss, and limits, deductibles, and policy terms affect what is available. California’s standard minimum liability limits increased to $30,000 per person and $60,000 per accident for bodily injury, plus $15,000 for property damage, on January 1, 2025. Those minimums do not establish the value of an injury claim. [California Department of Insurance auto coverage guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

### Do not overlook filing deadlines

California personal injury and wrongful-death claims often start with a two-year general filing rule, but that is not a universal deadline. Government claims, delayed discovery, minors, medical malpractice, and other exceptions require individual review. Property-damage deadlines may also differ. Do not wait for a citation to be resolved before checking a civil claim’s deadlines. [California Courts filing-deadline guidance](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/statute-limitations)

After a fatal collision, wrongful-death and survival actions are distinct claims with different available damages. They should not be treated as interchangeable. Legal review should address both the appropriate claim and its deadline.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there one minimum speed for every California freeway?

No. California’s general minimum-speed law does not establish one statewide minimum mph for every freeway. It focuses on unnecessary interference with normal, reasonable traffic, subject to safety and legal exceptions. Road signs and conditions still matter.

### Can I receive a ticket for driving below the posted limit?

Potentially, but driving below the maximum does not by itself establish a violation. The circumstances matter, including whether you unnecessarily impeded traffic and whether safety required a lower speed. A posted maximum does not require maintaining that speed in dangerous conditions.

### Should I speed up in heavy rain if traffic backs up behind me?

Not if doing so would be unsafe. Adjust your speed to visibility and traction. Drive predictably and allow passing when safe and lawful. Pressure from following traffic does not override safe-speed requirements.

### What if my vehicle suddenly cannot maintain speed?

Maintain control, communicate your intentions, and reach a safe location if possible. Use hazard lights when appropriate and request assistance. Avoid an unnecessary stop in an active lane, but do not keep driving an unsafe vehicle merely to match traffic.

### Does a minimum-speed ticket prove fault for a rear-end crash?

Not automatically. Following distance, visibility, braking, and each driver’s conduct still require investigation. Preserve footage, photographs, witness details, and relevant records so responsibility can be assessed from the evidence.

### What should I bring to a lawyer reviewing my citation?

Bring the citation, court notices, your written account, footage, photographs, and witness details. Include repair or towing records if mechanical trouble contributed. Ask about deadlines, procedures, fees, and realistic outcomes. An assessment should not promise dismissal before reviewing the facts.
