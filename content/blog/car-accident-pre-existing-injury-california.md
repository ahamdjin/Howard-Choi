## A prior condition and a new injury are not the same question

California’s civil jury instructions distinguish an existing condition from its aggravation. CACI 3927 addresses compensation for a condition made worse by another person’s wrongful conduct, rather than compensation for the earlier condition itself. CACI 3928 addresses a person who is unusually susceptible to injury: vulnerability does not excuse harm actually caused by the defendant. Both appear in the [Judicial Council’s California civil jury instructions](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

These principles do not decide what happened in an individual crash. You still need evidence of responsibility, a connection between the collision and the claimed harm, and support for the losses requested. A diagnosis that existed before the crash cannot simply be included in full as a new accident expense. Equally, an older diagnosis should not end the inquiry into what changed.

The [California personal injury guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-guide) explains the broader claim. This article focuses on the comparison that becomes important when an insurer or medical reviewer points to your history.

## Describe your baseline before describing the change

Start with what you could actually do before the collision. Were you working a normal schedule, receiving regular care, taking medication, or avoiding certain activities? If a symptom came and went, describe that pattern rather than saying it never existed. If you had recovered enough to resume a task, note when and what records support that account.

Then describe the change with the same level of detail. “My neck is worse” can be accurate, but it leaves unanswered questions. Explain whether the location, frequency or intensity changed; whether a symptom is new; and which activity became difficult. These are observations for a clinician to evaluate, not a diagnosis you need to make yourself.

If the earlier history is uncertain, say so and obtain the records. A confident but incorrect account can create an avoidable inconsistency later. The goal is a fair comparison, including limitations you already had, rather than an idealized account of life before the accident.

## Build a short before-and-after record

| Part of the comparison | Useful supporting material | What it can help clarify |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier symptoms and care | Relevant treatment notes, discharge records and medication history | What was present and how it was managed |
| Function before the collision | Work schedules, activity records and prior restrictions | Which tasks you were doing and any existing limits |
| First changes after impact | Initial medical notes, dated observations and follow-up appointments | When changes were noticed and reported |
| Continuing effects | Later evaluations, restrictions and treatment recommendations | How the course developed after the initial visit |
| Financial consequences | Bills, pay records and documented assistance | Which additional expenses or work losses are claimed |

Keep the dates and source of each record. A table you prepare helps organize the file, but it does not replace the underlying medical records or prove causation. If a relative supplies an observation, identify it as that person’s observation rather than presenting it as a medical finding.

## Give the treating clinician an accurate history

Tell your provider about relevant earlier injuries, surgeries, symptoms and treatment. Describe the collision and the changes you noticed, including symptoms that arose later. Follow clinical advice and ask medical questions about your condition and treatment needs. Do not seek a legal conclusion from someone who has not evaluated the issue.

A record may need clarification if it lists a symptom as new when you reported that it was an older symptom that became more frequent. Ask the provider about the office’s correction or addendum process. Preserve the original entry and any later clarification. An accurate addendum is different from rewriting history or removing an unfavorable note.

Do not delay necessary care to improve a claim, and do not continue unnecessary care to increase its value. If access, transportation or cost interrupted treatment, record what happened and discuss practical options with the provider. The [medical bills guide](/blogs/medical-bills-while-car-accident-claim-pending-california) can help you organize payment questions while the injury claim remains unresolved.

## How to handle imaging that mentions degeneration or an old injury

Read a scan report with the clinician responsible for interpreting its significance in your case. A phrase on an imaging report is not, by itself, an explanation of your prior symptoms, current functioning, or the cause of every complaint. Nor does a scan automatically prove that all symptoms began in the collision.

Ask which findings matter to the current evaluation and whether earlier studies are useful for comparison. If a prior scan exists, obtain the actual study and report through the appropriate records process when needed. Keep the dates clear so a reviewer does not accidentally compare studies in the wrong order.

Avoid trying to settle a medical disagreement by searching a single term online. The useful questions concern your history, examination, relevant testing and the clinician’s reasoning. Where the cause or extent of worsening is disputed, a legal evaluation may require an appropriately qualified medical opinion.

## A hypothetical example of aggravation

Consider someone who had intermittent lower-back symptoms before a rear-end crash. They were working full shifts and occasionally attending follow-up visits. After the crash, they report more frequent symptoms, receive a new work restriction and miss shifts. The earlier condition must be disclosed, and the claimed change needs support.

A useful file would include the relevant earlier notes, records of the work schedule, the first post-crash assessment, subsequent restrictions and proof of missed earnings. The reviewer can then examine what was already present, what changed and why the provider connects a particular limitation to the collision. This example illustrates record organization; it is not a firm case result or a prediction of compensation.

If the records show a restriction already existed, acknowledge it. The claim should explain the additional effect rather than attribute the entire restriction to the newer event. If symptoms later improved, include that improvement as well. A complete chronology helps avoid overstating either the earlier condition or the new harm.

## Responding when an adjuster says it was all pre-existing

Ask what part of the claim the insurer disputes. Is it questioning a particular appointment, a new symptom, time off work, future care, or the whole claimed injury? Request the evidence and explanation behind the position. You can respond more usefully to a defined disagreement than to a general reference to your history.

Identify the relevant comparison in the records and any missing information. An adjuster may have the earlier diagnosis but not the later restriction, or a bill without the accompanying clinical note. The [insurance adjuster guide](/blogs/what-is-an-insurance-adjuster-car-accident) explains how to track requests and submissions. If a decision denies benefits or a loss, the [denied insurance claim guide](/blogs/car-insurance-claim-denied-california) covers the next review steps.

Review the scope of medical authorizations and records requests. Ask about the providers, date range, purpose and recipients. Do not hide relevant history or assume every request should be ignored. Your obligations can differ depending on whose insurer is asking and whether litigation is underway. Obtain advice when you are unsure how to respond.

## Separate the additional losses you can support

Track treatment expenses, work losses and practical assistance with their dates and explanations. A new appointment may involve both an earlier condition and a change after the collision. Keep the records needed to explain the relationship rather than deciding that every bill belongs entirely in one category without medical support.

For time away from work, keep pay records and the actual restriction or schedule change. If you were already working reduced hours, use that earlier schedule as part of the comparison. For help at home, record the task, why help became necessary and who provided it. Distinguish a family member’s observation from a professional recommendation for future care.

The [injury claim value guide](/blogs/how-much-is-my-personal-injury-case-worth-california) explains why a settlement estimate depends on supported losses and other case facts. A pre-existing condition does not produce a standard percentage reduction, and a particular diagnosis does not create a guaranteed settlement amount.

If the injury creates long-term care or work concerns, the [serious injury practice page](/practice-areas/serious-injuries) explains the additional records that can help evaluate those needs. Keep an existing limitation separate from any newly recommended support so the review can identify the change you are claiming.

## More than one incident requires a clearer timeline

If another crash, workplace event or unrelated health change occurred, identify it accurately. Use separate dates, claim numbers and provider records. Tell your attorney about earlier claims and settlements that may be relevant, including any release you signed. Do not assume that using a different insurer makes the histories unrelated.

For a moped or scooter injury, preserve the device information alongside the medical chronology. The [moped and scooter guide](/blogs/mopeds-vs-scooters-california) explains why the vehicle category and trip records raise separate questions. Those records can help describe the event while medical evidence addresses the effect on your condition.

For a consultation about a Buena Park, Orange County or Los Angeles County collision, a short chronology is more useful than a pile of records with no dates. The [personal injury practice page](/practice-areas/personal-injury) describes the firm’s approach to evaluating a claim. Bring the documents you have and identify what is missing. Check [applicable injury deadlines](/blogs/california-personal-injury-deadlines) while records are being obtained; a complicated medical history is not a reason to assume extra filing time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I make a claim if I was already receiving treatment?

Possibly. Ongoing treatment does not by itself decide whether a crash caused additional harm. The file needs to distinguish the existing condition and care from any worsening or new injury, with appropriate medical support and evidence of the other party’s responsibility.

### What if I had an old diagnosis but no recent symptoms?

Explain that history accurately and obtain relevant records. A diagnosis and the symptoms or restrictions you experienced are different facts. Medical evaluation is needed to determine what the older finding means and whether the collision caused the current problems.

### Should I tell my lawyer about an earlier accident?

Yes. Provide the date, injuries, treatment, prior claim information and any relevant settlement documents. Early disclosure allows the review to address genuine overlap and inconsistent records. Hiding an earlier incident can make an already difficult causation question harder to evaluate.

### Does a pre-existing condition automatically reduce compensation?

No fixed reduction applies simply because a condition existed. The issue is the harm caused or aggravated by the defendant’s conduct and the evidence supporting it, along with other liability and coverage questions. The entire prior condition is not automatically recoverable either.

### Can I settle before the extent of worsening is clear?

Before signing a final release, understand the injuries and claims it covers, the current medical assessment and any supported future needs. Ask what remains uncertain and how settling would affect that uncertainty. A quick offer does not replace a review of the release and relevant medical evidence.
