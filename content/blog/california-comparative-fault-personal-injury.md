If someone has told you that you were partly to blame and therefore have no claim, that is wrong, and it is one of the more common things people are told by the other side's insurance company.

## California divides fault, it does not assign it

California uses pure comparative fault. Responsibility is split between everyone involved as a percentage, and your recovery is reduced by your share.

Found ten percent responsible on a claim worth 100,000 dollars? You recover 90,000. Found sixty percent responsible? You still recover forty percent. There is no cut-off at which being partly at fault wipes out the claim, which is not true in every state and is worth knowing if you have moved here or read advice written elsewhere.

## What that looks like in practice

Someone runs a red light and hits you. Clear enough. But you were driving a few miles an hour over the limit, and the other side argues that contributed.

Whether that argument goes anywhere depends on evidence, not assertion. Did the speed actually affect the collision? Is there anything showing it? A percentage that gets floated in a phone call is not the same as a percentage anyone could defend.

## The evidence that moves it

Scene photographs showing final vehicle positions and lane markings. Camera footage. Independent witnesses. The physical damage pattern, which often tells you more about angle and speed than any statement does. The police report, though it is worth knowing that an officer's opinion about fault is not binding on anyone.

## The adjuster's number is an opening position

This is the part worth internalising. When an insurer tells you that you were thirty percent at fault, that is one company's assessment, usually made early, often from a single phone call and a photograph.

It is not a ruling. It is not final. Fault assessments shift regularly when better evidence appears, and the initial figure tends to be the one most favourable to the insurer.

## It shapes strategy, not just arithmetic

Disputed fault changes how a claim should be handled. It raises the value of accident reconstruction, makes early evidence preservation more urgent, and affects whether a case realistically settles or needs to be filed.

It also affects what a reasonable settlement looks like. A claim with a genuine fault dispute carries risk for both sides, and both sides know it.

## If fault is being argued

Preserve everything about how the crash happened, not just your injuries. Photographs of the scene while it is still intact, the identity of anyone who saw it, any camera that may have caught it, and your own written account written down early while you still remember it clearly.

Be careful about agreeing to a version of events on a recorded call before you have thought it through. And do not accept a percentage simply because someone with an official-sounding job title asserted it.


## How a percentage affects recovery: an illustration, not a settlement quote

Assume an appropriately supported total damages figure of $100,000 and a hypothetical finding that the injured person was responsible for 30% of the harm. Reducing $100,000 by 30% leaves $70,000 as a simple mathematical illustration. That does not mean an insurer will pay $70,000: medical causation, available insurance, liens, eligible damages, procedural issues and collectability may still affect a real result.

Fault is not negotiated in a vacuum. A different interpretation of witness video or a traffic-control sequence could change the liability assessment while leaving the medical evidence identical. Keep the responsibility analysis and damages analysis separate so a discussion about traffic conduct does not hide uncertainty about actual losses.

## What can and cannot establish responsibility

An insurer may rely on a recorded statement, police report, vehicle damage or photographs. Those are potentially important sources, but none alone resolves every case. If a traffic light was involved, identify the signal phase and which direction each vehicle approached; if a rear-end collision occurred, ask what braking and following-distance evidence exists and whether an unexpected maneuver is claimed.

California civil jury instructions address negligence and comparative responsibility. How those rules apply depends on the proven facts, applicable traffic obligations and each person's conduct. Treat witness memory carefully, especially when a witness is inferring speed or signal color rather than describing what they directly observed.

## When more than two parties are involved

A collision can include a driver, vehicle owner, employer or a road-related issue, each with different potential legal responsibilities. A slip-and-fall might instead involve a tenant, property owner or maintenance contractor. The mere presence of multiple names does not establish liability; the investigator has to connect a specific act, responsibility and causal role to each party.

Insurance limits are also separate from legal fault. Two insurers may disagree about who should pay; the disagreement does not decide which person was negligent. Keep the identity, relationship and available policy records for each potentially relevant party.

## Questions to take into an early consultation

Ask what facts support the current fault percentage, what evidence could reasonably change it, whether all potential responsible parties are identified, and what deadlines govern evidence and filing. Bring the original scene photographs, report number, witness contacts and statements you have already provided to insurers. Be candid about uncertainty and any conduct on your own part.

A sound review will not promise that partial fault always disappears after a lawyer's involvement or that a jury must accept a preferred percentage. It should explain the range of plausible factual findings and the evidence needed to test them.
