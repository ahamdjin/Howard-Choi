## Electronic logging devices can show driving-time information

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration rules require many commercial drivers to use electronic logging devices to record hours-of-service information. An ELD synchronizes with the vehicle's engine and automatically records elements such as driving time, date and time, location, engine hours, vehicle miles, and identifying information.

Those records may help evaluate how long the driver had been operating the vehicle and whether the driver's recorded duty status is consistent with other evidence. An ELD does not answer every liability question by itself, but it can become an important part of the timeline.

## Hours-of-service records are only one part of the company file

Depending on the carrier and collision, useful records can also include dispatch information, trip documents, bills of lading, fuel or toll records, driver qualification materials, training records, prior safety information, inspection reports, and internal communications.

The relevant documents depend on the theory of the case. A fatigue issue calls for a different record set than a maintenance failure, improper loading, unsafe hiring, or a collision involving a delivery fleet.

## Maintenance and inspection records can matter

Commercial vehicles operate under maintenance and inspection requirements that can create records about the truck, trailer, brakes, tires, lights, and other equipment. When a mechanical condition may have contributed to the collision, those records can become more important than ordinary repair photographs.

The physical vehicle itself may also contain evidence. If a serious crash raises a mechanical issue, documenting the condition before repair, salvage, or destruction can matter.

## Cameras and electronic vehicle data may be time sensitive

Many commercial vehicles use dash cameras, driver-monitoring systems, telematics, GPS, collision-warning systems, or other electronic technology. Nearby businesses, other vehicles, and roadway systems may also hold video.

Retention periods vary. The fact that a record exists today does not mean it will still exist months later. Serious cases often require early identification of the evidence sources and a preservation strategy tailored to the carrier and vehicle involved.

## Federal retention rules create important timing questions

FMCSA materials explain that motor carriers must retain records of duty status and supporting documents for six months. Other categories of records can have different retention requirements, and companies may keep some information longer as a matter of practice.

The key point is not to assume that every useful commercial record will remain available indefinitely. If driving-time or company evidence could matter, the question should be addressed early.

## Ordinary crash evidence still matters

Truck-specific records do not replace scene photographs, police reports, witness statements, medical records, insurance information, or documentation of the injured person's losses. They supplement that evidence by showing what may have been happening within the commercial operation before the collision.

A strong investigation connects the company record to the crash record and then connects the crash to the medical and financial consequences that followed.

## Multiple companies can complicate responsibility and coverage

The driver, motor carrier, vehicle owner, trailer owner, shipper, broker, maintenance provider, employer, contractor, or another business may have different roles in a commercial transportation operation. Not every company is legally responsible in every case, but identifying the relationships is necessary before responsibility and insurance can be evaluated accurately.

That is why a commercial-truck claim should not automatically be treated as an ordinary auto claim with a larger vehicle. The evidence and insurance structure can be fundamentally different.


## An ELD is a record of duty status, not a universal crash recorder

Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration resources explain that electronic logging devices are designed to record drivers' hours-of-service information. A log may be relevant to driving time and compliance, but it is not automatically a video, a detailed vehicle dynamics recorder or proof of exactly how the collision occurred. Depending on the equipment, other electronic systems or video may provide different information.

A careful investigator should identify the actual devices and operators rather than assuming every truck has the same technology. Ask which records exist, which business controls them, the periods they cover and how they are normally retained. The existence or absence of a log does not by itself establish negligence.

## Who has the documents?

Commercial crashes may involve the driver, motor carrier, vehicle owner, trailer owner, dispatcher, employer or a maintenance contractor. Not all will possess the same evidence or owe the same legal duties. A delivery-van claim can differ materially from a tractor-trailer case subject to federal carrier rules.

Record identifying marks on the tractor and trailer, available USDOT numbers, business names, license plates, trailer number and approximate route. Keep the responding-agency report and witness details. These identifiers can help narrow where electronic records and insurance documents might be held.

## What a focused preservation request may concern

Potential categories include the relevant time window of driver logs, dispatch communications, vehicle inspection reports, maintenance and repair history, relevant camera files, driver qualification records and load-related documentation. The appropriate scope depends on the event; a blanket request for every company document is not a substitute for identifying the factual theory.

Records can have different retention periods under applicable law and company practice. Preserve your own copies of messages and images. For records held by another party, seek qualified advice promptly on appropriate preservation and access rather than attempting unauthorized retrieval.

## Medical complexity and collision complexity are different

A commercial truck's size can be relevant to forces involved, but injury causation still requires medical records and appropriate expert assessment where needed. Maintain a clear distinction between work restrictions, recommended future care, vehicle damage and legal fault.

Coverage layers, liability among multiple defendants and supported lifetime costs may each be evaluated separately. Do not assume the carrier is legally responsible for every event involving its branding or that a high policy limit produces a guaranteed payout.

## Which data is in an ELD—and which data usually is not?

FMCSA's ELD guidance describes a device that automatically records selected dates, times, location points, engine hours, vehicle miles and driver/carrier identifiers at specified events and intervals. The system documents hours of service; it does not automatically reproduce a video of the crash or precisely track every foot of travel. FMCSA explains that location data may be captured at hourly intervals while moving and at certain events such as an engine start, engine shutdown or duty-status change.

This distinction matters. Imagine an ELD shows the driver had been on duty for many hours, but the dispute concerns whether a truck changed lanes without checking a blind spot. The log may help reconstruct work history, while dashcam footage, nearby video, witness accounts, lane geometry and vehicle observations may speak more directly to the lane-change sequence. It would be unsound to claim the ELD itself proved the lane movement.

Conversely, an absence of an obvious hours-of-service violation does not prove the driver was alert or that the vehicle was mechanically sound. It answers only a portion of the investigation. Ask what equipment was present and what each device actually records before requesting 'the black box' as if every truck uses identical systems.

## Federal hours-of-service rules as context, not a verdict

For many property-carrying interstate drivers, FMCSA describes an 11-hour driving limit after at least ten consecutive hours off duty and a 14-hour on-duty window, with a qualifying 30-minute break from driving after eight cumulative driving hours without an appropriate interruption. Exceptions and special operating rules exist; California intrastate operations or specific short-haul services may require different analysis.

A useful timeline aligns the log with dispatch times, pickup and delivery documents, fuel receipts, toll events and relevant communication. If the electronic duty status says off duty while other records suggest the driver was working, the discrepancy deserves inquiry. It does not by itself tell you whether fatigue caused a collision.

Request examination of the actual regulatory category and any documented exceptions rather than counting hours from a screenshot. A reasoned analysis should show both the governing rule and how the underlying records support or contradict compliance.

## Different evidence has different retention periods

FMCSA states that motor carriers generally retain ELD records of duty status and supporting documents for six months under the applicable federal provisions. That does not mean every other type of commercial document has a six-month retention period. FMCSA's maintenance guidance describes different retention requirements, including records maintained for at least one year for vehicles within the regulatory scope and an additional six months after certain vehicles leave a carrier's control.

Private dashcams, telematics clips, accident-detection reports, company chat messages, cell-phone content and roadway surveillance can have entirely different storage practices. Even when a legal preservation duty may arise, the question of what existed, who held it and what reasonable steps were taken is fact-specific.

For that reason, identify the carrier, truck, trailer, dispatch provider and any separate camera service early. Preserve your own photographs and incident documents. When another party holds records, a qualified lawyer can evaluate appropriate preservation requests, discovery mechanisms and legal duties.

## A collision-specific truck evidence inventory

For the road scene, preserve the full truck and trailer plate information, USDOT or carrier identifying numbers if visible, trailer/container number, location, approximate time, approach direction, roadway geometry, weather, skid or debris evidence and witness contacts. Photograph the vehicles before repair when safe and lawful; a close-up of an emblem is not a substitute for images showing the truck's full configuration.

For the commercial operation, possible evidence includes duty-status records, dispatch timeline, bills of lading, relevant communications, trip sequence, vehicle inspections, maintenance and repair histories, driver qualification materials, onboard video and trailer-loading documents. Not every category exists in every case. The request should target a plausible issue, such as load shift, improper maintenance, unsafe driving or disputed employer control.

For injury and damages, preserve clinical records, treatment chronology, work restrictions, receipts and supported future-care recommendations. A detailed company-file investigation does not automatically establish the cause or extent of the injured person's medical condition.

## Worked example: lane merge near a commercial corridor

Suppose a tractor-trailer and sedan collide during a freeway lane merge. The sedan driver believes the truck encroached; the carrier says the sedan entered a blind spot. The key question is not simply who owns the larger vehicle. It is what movements occurred and whether either driver failed to use reasonable care.

Time-stamped dashcam footage, a witness account, roadway markings, tire or damage patterns and available vehicle data may clarify the actual movement. The truck's ELD could independently help identify the driver, event timing and work period, but its ordinary duties-of-service data should not be presented as a camera substitute.

If investigation identifies a specific loading, maintenance or dispatch issue, additional companies may need to be considered. Ask what those entities controlled, what actions are supported by records, and which insurer represents which alleged responsibility. Keep the example as a question framework, not a declaration that all carriers are liable for their drivers or trailers.

## Answers to common truck-record questions

Can you demand the ELD record directly from the driver at the scene? You may ask what information exists, but access and preservation obligations are governed by applicable law and procedure. Never access another party's electronic account or equipment without authorization.

Does the truck's insurance policy establish the value of an injury? No. Coverage, legal responsibility, medical causation and supported damages are separate questions. Multiple policy layers may be relevant but should be confirmed, not guessed.

What original sources support this discussion? FMCSA's ELD technical FAQs, its six-month ELD retention guidance, the summary of hours-of-service rules, and the federal motor-carrier maintenance records guidance. Those source links accompany this guide for independent review.


## Match the requested record to the disputed question

An hours-of-service dispute calls for different records from a brake-maintenance dispute. List the question first: fatigue, vehicle condition, driver supervision, cargo movement or the actual collision sequence. Next identify the potentially relevant record, the likely custodian and what is known about retention. Do not assume the truck driver personally controls dispatch records or that every vehicle stores the same electronic information.

Keep a preservation request distinct from obtaining the material. Record what was requested, whether the recipient acknowledged it, and what still requires follow-up or formal process. If a truck or component may be inspected, ask about a coordinated protocol before repair, disposal or invasive testing. This prevents a long list of requested documents from being mistaken for a completed investigation and helps the lawyer prioritize evidence that could actually affect responsibility.
