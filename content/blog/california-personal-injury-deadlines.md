Two years. That is the answer most people are looking for, and for a straightforward California injury claim it is usually right. The reason this needs a whole article is that the exceptions are not obscure edge cases, they are ordinary situations that come up constantly.

## The general rule

California gives an injured person two years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit. Filing means starting a court case, not opening an insurance claim, and that distinction catches people out.

## Government claims run on months, not years

If a city, county, transit agency, school district or state body may be responsible, a much shorter clock applies. That can mean a government claim has to be presented well before the two-year mark, and missing it can end an otherwise good case entirely.

This comes up more than people expect: a city-owned vehicle, a public bus, a poorly maintained road or intersection, a county hospital. If any public entity is in the picture, treat the deadline as urgent from day one rather than something to check later.

## Minors and delayed discovery

Where the injured person is a child, the clock generally does not run in the usual way while they are a minor. Separately, some injuries are not discovered immediately, and California recognizes that in limited circumstances.

Both are genuinely fact-specific. They are worth raising early rather than assuming either does or does not apply to you.

## Property damage is its own timeline

Damage to your car and injury to your body are not necessarily on the same clock. People sometimes resolve the vehicle quickly, assume the whole matter is closed, and are surprised later.

## Evidence has a deadline nobody writes down

This is the practical one. Long before any legal deadline, the things that prove your case start disappearing.

Nearby camera footage often overwrites within days. Vehicle data can be lost when a car is repaired or salvaged. Witnesses move and become unreachable. A commercial vehicle's electronic records are kept only as long as the rules require, and then legitimately deleted.

You can be comfortably inside the two-year window and have already lost the evidence that would have won the case.

## Talking to the insurer does not pause anything

A common and costly misunderstanding. Negotiations with an insurance company do not extend the filing deadline. An adjuster who is being slow, friendly and apparently cooperative is not stopping your clock, and the deadline arriving mid-negotiation does not entitle you to more time.

## What determines your actual date

The date of injury, who the potential defendants are, whether any of them is a public entity, the age of the injured person, and the specific facts of how and when the harm was discovered.

Work it out from those rather than from the general rule, and do it early. Nothing in this area rewards waiting.


## Three deadlines that should never be treated as one

Evidence may disappear first: footage may overwrite, a vehicle may be repaired and witnesses may become unreachable. Insurers may impose contractual notice or documentation duties that are separate from the court filing period. A civil statute of limitations is the legal time limit to commence an action, not a promise that evidence or insurance rights can wait until then.

Create a timeline with the injury date, all potentially responsible parties, dates of medical care, date any public entity may have been identified, insurer notices and the anticipated lawsuit deadline. Each entry needs a source and should be checked with a professional where the legal rule is uncertain.

## Government claims: the short administrative clock

California Government Code section 911.2 generally requires injury-related claims against a public entity to be presented within six months of accrual, subject to the precise claim and exceptions. This preliminary claim process is separate from filing a lawsuit. A crash involving a government-owned vehicle, public roadway or other public service can raise the question even when a private individual was also involved.

Identifying the proper public entity and place for presentment is not always obvious. Whether a road is city, county or state controlled may need verification, and special late-claim procedures have limits. An ordinary personal-injury article should not tell readers that two years is safely available in every such case.

## Minors, delayed discovery and other exceptions

Legal rules for minors and delayed-discovery situations can change the calculation in some kinds of injury matters, but the exception may be limited or inapplicable depending on the defendant and underlying claim. Do not assume a child's claim can safely wait until adulthood, especially where government-claim rules or evidence preservation are involved.

Serious injuries that take months to treat do not automatically extend the filing period. Legal advice about timing should begin with who the potential defendants are, the date and manner of harm, and the precise rule governing each claim.

## What happens while insurance negotiations continue?

Insurer conversations, promises to review bills and ongoing settlement offers ordinarily do not stop the legal clock by themselves. Preserve every letter and email with its date and who sent it, including any written agreement concerning time limits. Never infer an extension from the adjuster's friendly tone.

Ask a lawyer to confirm the actual filing deadline and whether any administrative or arbitration process is involved. The relevant question is not simply 'How long do I have to sue?' but 'Which actions must occur, with which parties, by which dates, to preserve this particular claim?'
