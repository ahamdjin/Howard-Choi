Two years. That is the answer most people are looking for, and for a straightforward California injury claim it is usually right. The reason this needs a whole article is that the exceptions are not obscure edge cases, they are ordinary situations that come up constantly.

## The general rule

California gives an injured person two years from the date of injury to file a personal injury lawsuit. Filing means starting a court case, not opening an insurance claim, and that distinction catches people out.

## Government claims run on months, not years

If a city, county, transit agency, school district or state body may be responsible, a much shorter clock applies. That can mean a government claim has to be presented well before the two-year mark, and missing it can end an otherwise good case entirely.

Possible examples include a city-owned vehicle, a public transit vehicle or an allegedly dangerous public roadway. These situations may raise additional immunity, notice and substantive-liability rules, not just a shorter deadline. If a public entity may be involved, promptly identify the responsible agency and ask about the exact government-claim requirements.

## Minors and delayed discovery

Where the injured person is a child, the clock generally does not run in the usual way while they are a minor. Separately, some injuries are not discovered immediately, and California recognizes that in limited circumstances.

Both are genuinely fact-specific. They are worth raising early rather than assuming either does or does not apply to you.

## Property damage is its own timeline

Damage to your car and injury to your body are not necessarily on the same clock. People sometimes resolve the vehicle quickly, assume the whole matter is closed, and are surprised later.

## Evidence has a deadline nobody writes down

This is the practical one. Long before any legal deadline, the things that prove your case start disappearing.

Nearby camera footage often overwrites within days. Vehicle data can be lost when a car is repaired or salvaged. Witnesses move and become unreachable. A commercial vehicle's electronic records are kept only as long as the rules require, and then legitimately deleted.

You can be comfortably inside the two-year window and have already lost the evidence that would have won the case.

## Talking to the insurer does not pause anything

A common and costly misunderstanding. Negotiations with an insurance company do not extend the filing deadline. An adjuster who is being slow, friendly and apparently cooperative is not stopping your clock, and the deadline arriving mid-negotiation does not entitle you to more time.

## What determines your actual date

The date of injury, who the potential defendants are, whether any of them is a public entity, the age of the injured person, and the specific facts of how and when the harm was discovered.

Work it out from those rather than from the general rule, and do it early. Nothing in this area rewards waiting.


## Three deadlines that should never be treated as one

Evidence may disappear first: footage may overwrite, a vehicle may be repaired and witnesses may become unreachable. Insurers may impose contractual notice or documentation duties that are separate from the court filing period. A civil statute of limitations is the legal time limit to commence an action, not a promise that evidence or insurance rights can wait until then.

Create a timeline with the injury date, all potentially responsible parties, dates of medical care, date any public entity may have been identified, insurer notices and the anticipated lawsuit deadline. Each entry needs a source and should be checked with a professional where the legal rule is uncertain.

## Government claims: the short administrative clock

California Government Code section 911.2 generally requires injury-related claims against a public entity to be presented within six months of accrual, subject to the precise claim and exceptions. This preliminary claim process is separate from filing a lawsuit. A crash involving a government-owned vehicle, public roadway or other public service can raise the question even when a private individual was also involved.

Identifying the proper public entity and place for presentment is not always obvious. Whether a road is city, county or state controlled may need verification, and special late-claim procedures have limits. An ordinary personal-injury article should not tell readers that two years is safely available in every such case.

## Minors, delayed discovery and other exceptions

Legal rules for minors and delayed-discovery situations can change the calculation in some kinds of injury matters, but the exception may be limited or inapplicable depending on the defendant and underlying claim. Do not assume a child's claim can safely wait until adulthood, especially where government-claim rules or evidence preservation are involved.

Serious injuries that take months to treat do not automatically extend the filing period. Legal advice about timing should begin with who the potential defendants are, the date and manner of harm, and the precise rule governing each claim.

## What happens while insurance negotiations continue?

Insurer conversations, promises to review bills and ongoing settlement offers ordinarily do not stop the legal clock by themselves. Preserve every letter and email with its date and who sent it, including any written agreement concerning time limits. Never infer an extension from the adjuster's friendly tone.

Ask a lawyer to confirm the actual filing deadline and whether any administrative or arbitration process is involved. The relevant question is not simply 'How long do I have to sue?' but 'Which actions must occur, with which parties, by which dates, to preserve this particular claim?'

## A California deadline table in plain language

California Courts lists a general two-year period for most personal-injury lawsuits and three years for many property-damage lawsuits. That difference matters after a car accident because the cost of repairing a vehicle and the consequences of an injury are not necessarily part of the same legal clock. Neither period is an insurer's deadline to decide a claim.

For many claims against a California state or local public entity involving personal injury or damage to personal property, the Government Claims Act generally requires presenting an administrative claim within six months of accrual. Other categories may have different periods, and exceptions and late-claim procedures can apply only under specific conditions. California Courts explains that after an agency sends a written rejection, there is generally a six-month window to file suit; different rules can apply when no rejection is sent. Do not calculate one date and assume it covers every type of claim against every defendant.

The most helpful first question is not 'How many years do I have?' It is 'Whom might this claim be against, what is the legal basis, and which required step comes first?' The wrong defendant, missed claim-presentment procedure or mistaken accrual date can affect the result.

## A timeline example: public transit versus a private driver

Imagine a collision involving a privately owned car and a city-operated transit vehicle. A claimant might focus on the usual two-year personal-injury filing rule because it is easy to find online. But a claim against the public entity operating the transit service can trigger a much earlier administrative presentation deadline. The specific facts, agency and timing need review even when the other vehicle was privately owned.

Now imagine the same crash involved only two private motorists and no government actor. The general personal-injury period might be the starting point, but policy notices, preservation, insurance communications and legal issues particular to the case still require earlier action. The examples contrast types of deadlines; they do not establish the actual filing date for an individual case.

## Minor children and delayed discovery are not automatic extensions

California law has tolling and accrual doctrines for certain circumstances, including some claims involving minors or later-discovered harm. They are not universal permission to wait. A public-entity claim, medical-negligence dispute, contractual notice provision or other special statute can alter the analysis. Nor does the fact that medical treatment continues for a year automatically move the accident date forward by a year.

Record the birth date of a minor claimant, event date, first known injury symptoms, date a possible public defendant was identified, and any unusual delay in discovering what caused harm. Ask qualified counsel to identify the actual legal basis for an exception and record the advice clearly. Do not assume a relative's or adjuster's verbal statement extends a court deadline.

## The eight-column deadline worksheet

Make one row for each claim or defendant with these eight items: possible responsible person or entity; legal theory being considered; date of injury or relevant event; earliest potentially applicable notice or claim-presentment date; date a claim was sent; evidence of delivery; next agency or insurer response date; and the court filing deadline after legal review. If one field is unknown, mark it unknown instead of filling in a guessed date.

For government claims, preserve a copy of the form submitted and confirmation of how and when the agency received it. For litigation, preserve any written tolling agreement or lawyer communication separately from routine claim correspondence. A calendar reminder is useful, but a calendar does not decide whether a claim is timely.

Keep two independent reminders as well: one for evidence that might disappear in days or weeks, and one for insurance conditions that can require prompt notice or cooperation. Neither reminder substitutes for getting a legal date calculation from someone who can apply the correct statute to your facts.

## Questions people ask about California injury filing deadlines

Does opening a claim with State Farm or another insurer count as filing a lawsuit? No. Contacting an insurer may be necessary under a policy, but it is not the same as commencing a civil action in court.

Does a government agency's silence mean the claim is approved? No. California Courts describes separate rules for agency response and the period to sue after different types of response. The exact consequence depends on the agency process and facts; silence should not be treated as a payment promise or waiver.

What if the collision happened outside Buena Park? The law governing the event and the relevant defendants matters more than which city houses your lawyer. If the incident took place outside California, a different state's rules may apply; do not transplant these California examples.

Which official guidance should I read? California Courts' pages on statutes of limitations and government claims are the primary starting points; the latter points readers to California Government Code sections 905 and 911.2. Use those materials to ask the right questions, then obtain a specific deadline determination.


## Build a deadline register that explains its assumptions

Use separate rows for the accident, each potentially responsible person or entity, insurance notices, preservation requests, administrative claims and court filings. Record the date, the source of that date, who is responsible for checking it, and whether the deadline is confirmed or still needs legal review. A calendar entry without those assumptions can conceal the fact that the wrong event or defendant was used.

Keep proof of what was sent, to whom, by which permitted method and when. A draft on a computer, an unanswered voicemail or an insurer saying it opened a file is not the same evidence as a properly completed filing. Ask counsel to identify the next procedural step as well as the first date; meeting one requirement does not establish that every later requirement has been met. This register is an organizing tool, not a substitute for an individualized deadline assessment.
