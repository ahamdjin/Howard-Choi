## Where MedPay fits when more than one insurer is involved

After a collision, one bill can involve a provider, health plan, auto insurer, and possibly the other driver’s insurer. MedPay can be a source of payment under an auto policy while a liability claim is still being investigated. An injury claim against the other driver, by contrast, depends on responsibility and legally recoverable losses. The two processes can move at different speeds.

If bills are arriving before the fault investigation is complete, the [guide to medical bills while a claim is pending](/blogs/medical-bills-while-car-accident-claim-pending-california) explains how to organize coverage questions, provider balances and collection notices together.

If you are injured as a passenger, ask the carrier which person and vehicle the policy covers. The limit is often described per person, and the exact terms matter. A household member injured in a different car or a person injured outside a vehicle can raise additional coverage questions. Get a written explanation based on the policy rather than assuming that every family member or every crash qualifies.

MedPay generally addresses eligible medical expenses. It does not normally compensate for lost income, damage to your vehicle, or pain and suffering. Those losses can belong to separate claims. Keep them in the file even if a MedPay payment has already been made. The [California injury case value guide](/blogs/how-much-is-my-personal-injury-case-worth-california) explains how an injury claim can include losses beyond an immediate medical bill.

## Check the actual coverage before submitting bills

Look for medical payments coverage on the declarations page and obtain the policy or endorsement that describes it. Find the limit, who qualifies as insured, and any time period for covered expenses or submitting documents. Ask whether the carrier wants itemized bills, treatment dates, proof of payment, and a health plan explanation of benefits. Keep the insurer’s instructions in writing.

The amount on a statement can differ from the amount still due. A provider may bill an initial charge, the health plan may apply an adjustment, and part of the balance may be the patient’s responsibility. Ask for a current statement if you see a credit or another payment on the account. Avoid submitting the original charge as an outstanding balance after another payer has addressed it.

If the insurer asks for a broader medical authorization, ask what it needs to establish the eligibility of the specific bill. Compare the providers, date range and categories of records it requests. If it denies a bill, find out whether the issue is timing, eligibility, documentation, or a policy exclusion. Each reason calls for a different response.

If the review concerns an earlier condition, compare the relevant treatment history with the changes after this crash. The [pre-existing injury guide](/blogs/car-accident-pre-existing-injury-california) explains how to organize that comparison without treating every older symptom as a new injury.

## A two-passenger example

Suppose a driver and passenger both go to urgent care after a crash. The policy lists MedPay, and each has a separate bill. The carrier opens a file under the driver’s name. Before sending both sets of records to the same number, ask whether the passenger needs a separate claim reference and whether the limit applies separately to each person.

Next, suppose the passenger’s health plan has already paid part of one bill. Give the MedPay adjuster the current provider statement and the health plan’s explanation. Ask how the policy coordinates with that payment and where any approved MedPay amount will go. If the carrier pays the provider directly, obtain confirmation from both the carrier and the provider before paying the same bill yourself.

Now suppose the other driver’s insurer later proposes a settlement. Update the ledger before reviewing the offer. Note what was billed, what each payer paid, and who says money remains due. Review whether any policy or plan claims reimbursement from the settlement. A payment that helped you in the first month may affect the final distribution later, depending on the terms and law that apply.

## How MedPay differs from other coverage

| Coverage or payment | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| MedPay | Insured person, eligible expense, limit and paperwork |
| Health insurance | Network rules, adjustments, patient balance and plan terms |
| Other driver’s liability coverage | Responsibility, injury evidence, available limits and release terms |
| Uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage | Applicable policy terms, status of the other driver, and required notice or proof |

California does not operate a mandatory no-fault system for private auto injury claims. A medical payment made without determining fault does not change that. The [California fault and insurance guide](/blogs/is-california-a-no-fault-state-or-an-at-fault-state) explains the distinction between an available first-party benefit and a claim against a driver who caused the crash.

If the other driver is uninsured or has a small limit, ask about your uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage in addition to MedPay. The two benefits may have different eligibility requirements and may interact under the policy. Keep track of what has been paid and ask how any proposed resolution accounts for it. See the [uninsured motorist claims guide](/blogs/california-uninsured-driver-hit-and-run-injury-claims).

## Make one list you can reconcile with your insurer

- Enter the provider, treatment date, amount charged and amount currently due.
- Track health plan payments, provider adjustments, amounts you paid and MedPay payments in separate columns.
- Keep each bill and explanation of benefits next to its ledger entry.
- Note which expenses the auto insurer still needs to review and when you provided the documents.
- Save any reimbursement request and ask which payments it includes.

A letter saying a benefit has been exhausted can be checked against that list. Ask for the payment history and compare it with provider records. If something is missing or posted twice, identify the provider and date instead of disputing the entire balance. ## How to reconcile the insurer’s ledger with your provider

Ask the insurer whether an approved payment is going to you, the medical provider or another recipient. If the provider says the balance is unpaid, ask for the account ledger and compare the service date, amount and check or transaction number. A payment may have been issued but not yet posted. If the insurer and provider have different records, send the documents showing the mismatch and ask who will reconcile it.

Keep a copy of any check or electronic payment notice. If it covers more than one visit, ask the insurer how it allocated the amount. If the provider applies it to a different appointment, request a corrected statement. When an insurer says the per-person limit has been reached, a line-by-line history helps you see what was charged to the limit and whether the insurer processed a bill twice or assigned it to the wrong person.

### If a bill is denied because it arrived late

Find out whether the carrier disputes the date of treatment, the date you gave notice, or the date it received the bill. Those are separate events. Request the specific policy term and keep proof of the original submission. If the doctor sent the bill directly, ask the office when it sent it and how. If the insurer could not identify the claim because the number was missing, resubmit the bill with the correct reference and ask how that affects its review.

For a broader review of the decision, the [denied insurance claim guide](/blogs/car-insurance-claim-denied-california) explains how to match each stated reason with the records needed for a response.

### What to ask before you resolve the injury claim

Ask whether any plan or insurer has requested repayment and obtain the amount and basis in writing. Compare the request with the payment history. If one provider is still billing you for an amount the health plan has adjusted, verify the remaining balance before relying on it in settlement discussions. A proposal can appear to cover every expense while leaving a reimbursement issue open.

Keep your current ledger with any proposed release and the related correspondence. Confirm whether the release would close a liability claim, an insurance benefit, or both. If there is a dispute over unpaid treatment or a request for repayment, identify the payer and the specific bill. A useful review starts with those documents instead of an assumption that every payment will be treated the same way.

If the expense is for transport from the scene, request the transport provider’s itemized bill as well as the hospital statement. Different providers can bill separately for the same day. Confirm that the insurer received both before treating an incomplete payment as a denial.

## A balance check before you make a payment

Consider a hypothetical account with an initial $1,200 charge. The provider posts a $400 adjustment, a $500 health plan payment and a $200 MedPay payment. If those entries all apply to the same bill and there are no other charges or credits, the remaining balance is $100. Paying the original $1,200 statement would ignore three entries already on the account. Ask the provider for a statement showing how it reached the current balance.

Now suppose the MedPay carrier lists the $200 as paid, but the provider has no record of receiving it. The arithmetic alone cannot resolve that disagreement. Check who received the payment, whether it cleared, and whether the provider assigned it to another visit. Keep the disputed entry marked as unresolved instead of assuming the patient owes it or that it has been paid twice.

This exercise checks an account balance. It does not determine whether a payer can later seek reimbursement from an injury settlement. For that separate question, compare the request with the policy and payment history. The [subrogation guide](/blogs/what-is-subrogation-car-insurance-california) explains why recovery of vehicle payments and reimbursement of medical benefits need separate records.

If the same crash also left you without a car, keep those expenses outside the medical ledger. The [rental reimbursement guide](/blogs/rental-car-reimbursement-after-accident-california) covers daily limits, authorization dates and transportation invoices. Separate worksheets let you check each benefit without confusing a medical limit with a vehicle allowance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can MedPay help if I caused the crash?

It can pay eligible medical expenses regardless of fault when the policy provides coverage. Verify who is insured and which expenses qualify.

### Is MedPay required in California?

It is an optional coverage. Look for it on the declarations for the date of the crash and ask the insurer for the terms. Carrying liability insurance does not show that you purchased MedPay.

### Does MedPay replace health insurance?

It addresses particular accident-related expenses under an auto policy. Ask both payers how they handle the bill, and check for limits, adjustments and reimbursement terms.

### Can I still make a claim against the driver who caused the crash?

A MedPay payment does not by itself resolve a liability claim. Document the injury, identify other losses, and check any coordination or reimbursement requirements before accepting a settlement.

### What should I do if my provider sends a collection notice?

Contact the billing office promptly, identify the disputed bill and request a current ledger. Tell the auto insurer what notice you received and ask about any payment still under review. Ask the provider whether it will pause collection while the account is reconciled and get its response in writing. An open insurance claim alone does not establish that collection has stopped.
