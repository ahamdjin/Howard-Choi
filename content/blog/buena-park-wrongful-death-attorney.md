## What qualifies as wrongful death in California?

A wrongful death claim can arise when another person’s wrongful act or neglect causes a death. A fatal traffic collision, an unsafe condition on a property, or a defective product can prompt an investigation. The circumstances determine which legal theory applies and who might be responsible. [California Code of Civil Procedure § 377.60](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-2/title-3/chapter-4/article-6/section-377-60/)

A family may know exactly where the incident occurred while still lacking a clear account of what caused it. A crash report, for example, can document the location and the people involved. Photographs, witness accounts, vehicle information, and medical records can help answer the separate question of how the collision led to the death. [California Courts personal injury guidance](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/personal-injury)

## Who can bring the claim?

California’s statute identifies eligible claimants. They can include a surviving spouse, domestic partner, children, or other people who qualify under the statute’s heirship or dependency provisions. A personal representative can also assert the claim on behalf of eligible people. If family relationships are complicated, bring a list of potentially eligible relatives or dependents to the first consultation.

A wrongful death action concerns losses suffered by eligible survivors. A survival action concerns a claim that belonged to the person who died and proceeds through an authorized representative or successor. That distinction can affect who acts and which losses belong in which claim.

## What a lawyer can help establish

Start with a dated account. Write down what you know about the incident and where each detail came from. Keep original photographs, messages, bills, and letters in one place. If a camera may have recorded the incident, note who controls it and where it points. An attorney can assess whether to request that the recording be preserved.

Document the person’s contributions to the household as well. A pay stub captures one part of the picture. Regular childcare, transportation, or household work may matter too. A family can describe what the person did in a typical week and collect records that support that account.

Deadlines deserve early attention. California Courts describes a general two-year period for many injury and wrongful death actions. Claims involving a public entity can require an earlier administrative filing. Use our [California filing-deadline guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-deadlines) to prepare questions, then ask a lawyer to calculate deadlines from the actual facts, especially when multiple potential defendants are involved. ## What the first weeks of a claim can involve

An early investigation often starts with records that are easiest to lose. Camera footage can be overwritten, and people who saw an incident may leave the area without giving their contact details. Write down where a recording might exist and identify anyone who mentioned having seen it. Leave requests for sensitive or restricted material to counsel.

As records come in, build the timeline around events that can be documented. The time of an emergency call, the first hospital record, and a later communication from an insurer each establish a different event. Mark what you learned from a record and what someone remembers. That distinction gives an attorney a way to check discrepancies without asking the family to decide which account is correct on its own.

A consultation can also help clarify damages. Families commonly ask about funeral expenses, financial support, and the loss of the person’s care and companionship. Write down examples of what changed after the death. Counsel can explain which losses the law recognizes, what documents could support them, and what to expect if a claim proceeds through settlement discussions or litigation.

## How to organize evidence without getting ahead of the facts

Build the timeline around events you can date. Include the incident, the emergency response, any hospital transfers, and the date of death. Next to each entry, note the document or person it came from. If two people remember the time differently, save both accounts. A discrepancy may point to a record worth obtaining, such as a dispatch log or a timestamped camera clip.

If a business or public agency may have footage, write down the camera location, the direction it faced, and the window of time that matters. A lawyer can assess how to request preservation and whether a formal records request or court process is appropriate. Save original files you already have, along with the messages that show when and how you received them.

Keep a separate folder for the incident report, the death certificate if available, insurance correspondence, and financial records. Let counsel know whether there was an employer, commercial vehicle, property manager, or public agency involved. The person present at the scene and the organization responsible for a vehicle or premises can raise different questions about responsibility.

## What a family can document about its loss

Think through an ordinary week before the death. Who drove the children to school, prepared meals, paid household bills, or cared for a relative? Write down examples in your own words. A household calendar, receipts, bank records, or a family member's account can help explain what changed. The purpose is to give counsel enough detail to distinguish expenses already incurred from ongoing losses that may need more support.

If family members paid different expenses, record who paid each one and keep the receipt or invoice. Funeral arrangements, time away from work, and changes in household care can generate different kinds of records. Ask counsel which items may be recoverable and which belong in a separate claim. A personal record of what happened is a starting point for that review.

## What changes when a public entity may be involved

If the facts suggest a city vehicle, a public facility, or a dangerous condition on public property, flag that possibility promptly. California Courts explains that claims against government agencies commonly require an administrative claim before a lawsuit and can involve earlier deadlines. Keep any response or rejection notice and record the date it was mailed. The applicable deadline depends on the parties, facts, and type of claim. [California Courts government claims guide](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/government-claim)

Imagine a crash involving a privately owned truck at an intersection where a traffic signal was being repaired. The truck driver's actions and the work at the intersection raise separate questions. Document what you know about the vehicle and the signal work, then let counsel assess which parties and procedures are relevant. Early records collection can run alongside those legal checks.

Ask the attorney to identify the immediate tasks, the information still missing, and when you should expect an update. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can a sibling bring a wrongful death claim?

It depends on the family relationships and the eligibility provisions that apply. Review the full family circumstances under § 377.60 before assuming any relative can file.

### Should each family member start a separate case?

Get advice about coordinating eligible claimants before filing. Give counsel the names of people who may qualify and disclose any related proceedings.

### Does a criminal investigation prevent a civil claim?

Criminal and civil proceedings address different questions. Ask counsel about the civil deadline even if investigators are still gathering evidence.

### What if a city vehicle or public property was involved?

Tell counsel as early as possible. Public entity claims can have separate notice rules and shorter deadlines.

### Can I speak with a lawyer before receiving every record?

Yes. Bring what you have, identify what is missing, and note any approaching response dates. A lawyer can help prioritize the records that are likely to matter. You can [request a consultation](/contact) with the records currently available.
