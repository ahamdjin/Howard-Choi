## What a deductible looks like on a repair bill

Suppose the approved cost to fix your car is $3,800 and your collision deductible is $500. In this example, your own carrier pays $3,300 and you are responsible for the remaining $500. The shop may collect your portion at pickup. If the repair estimate later goes up because the shop finds hidden damage, ask how the supplement will be paid and whether it is part of the same loss. The original estimate can change without creating a new claim.

The deductible may be taken out of a payment. A total-loss statement might show the value, applicable taxes and fees, the deductible, and the net payment. A repair invoice might show what the insurer pays directly and what the shop expects you to pay. Ask for both the gross amount and the net amount so you can reconcile the documents.

A separate unpaid charge may look like another deductible when it is actually a disputed repair, an excluded item, or a bill the insurer has not reviewed. If the shop says you owe $850 when your deductible is $500, ask for a line item showing where the extra $350 came from. Compare the shop’s invoice with the insurer’s estimate and ask [the claims adjuster](/blogs/what-is-an-insurance-adjuster-car-accident) which charge the carrier has yet to approve.

## Which deductibles are relevant after a crash?

Collision and comprehensive coverage are separate. Collision ordinarily concerns damage from an impact, while comprehensive addresses covered events such as theft or vandalism. They can have different deductible amounts. The same vehicle may carry both, but the facts determine which coverage the insurer considers. Ask how the insurer classified the event and get the explanation in writing if it is disputed. [California Department of Insurance’s auto coverage guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

When you file a liability claim with the other driver’s insurer, your own collision deductible generally does not apply to that claim. The other insurer may still dispute coverage, fault, or the amount of the loss. Waiting for it can mean more time without the car, and it can introduce towing or storage questions. Compare the actual cost of waiting with the deductible on a claim you could open under your own coverage.

For a crash involving an identified uninsured driver, check whether you bought a collision deductible waiver and whether the facts qualify. The rules for a waiver, uninsured motorist property damage coverage, and a collision claim are different. If the driver left and cannot be identified, ask specifically what your policy requires for each benefit. See the [California uninsured driver guide](/blogs/california-uninsured-driver-hit-and-run-injury-claims).

## If the other driver caused the crash, can you get it back?

After paying under your policy, your insurer may try to recover from the responsible party. That is subrogation. California’s Department of Insurance says that when the insurer pursues that recovery, it generally includes your deductible in the process. Ask whether the company intends to pursue recovery and how it will calculate your share if it receives only part of what it seeks. A pending recovery is not a payment you can count on when picking up the car. [California Department of Insurance’s accident guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/hadaccident.cfm)

If your insurer will not pursue it, ask for that decision in writing and clarify your options before contacting the other party. Do not sign an agreement releasing the other driver in exchange for the deductible without understanding what else the agreement covers. It could affect the insurer’s recovery or your own claim for injuries or other expenses. Check both the amount and the scope of any offer.

### Compare two paths with the same repair estimate

Suppose a repair estimate is $5,600. Your carrier says it can inspect the car now and you have a $750 deductible. The other driver’s company says it has opened a file but still needs to speak with its driver. Ask your carrier how it will handle the deductible and when you will owe it. Ask the other carrier when it will make the next decision and whether it has authorized towing, storage, or a rental.

The answer can change the decision even if both routes eventually pay the same repair amount. If you need the car for work and the shop is ready, a known $750 cost today may be easier to manage than an open-ended delay. If the other carrier has already accepted fault and confirmed repairs, using your policy may add an unnecessary step. Confirm the details in writing before authorizing work.

## Choosing a deductible before the next claim

Compare the premium savings with the additional amount you would have to pay for a covered loss. For example, if changing from a $500 deductible to $1,000 saves $120 a year, you would take on $500 more out-of-pocket exposure for that coverage. One claim during the next four years can consume most of that premium savings. Actual quotes and losses vary, so treat the calculation as a comparison rather than a prediction.

Ask a lender or leasing company whether it limits the deductible you can carry. If the vehicle is old or worth little, compare the cost of maintaining the coverage with the amount that could be paid after the deductible. Keep emergency funds in mind. A lower monthly premium can be hard to justify if the household cannot pay the deductible when repairs are needed.

## Check the written amount before you approve repairs

- Which coverage is paying, and how much is its deductible?
- Will the insurer subtract it from the check or will the shop collect it?
- Are there separate expenses or estimate differences that the insurer has not agreed to pay?
- Will the insurer pursue recovery, and what updates will it provide?
- If the car is totaled, what value, taxes, fees, and deductions appear on the settlement statement?

If the crash also caused injuries, set up a separate record for the medical and work losses. A deductible issue belongs to a particular coverage. It does not decide what an injury claim is worth. ## How to check a deductible refund

Get the original repair bill and the amount your carrier actually paid. Then compare the recovery letter with those two figures. If the carrier says it recovered only part of the claim, ask how it allocated that money between you and the insurer. Check whether the letter describes a gross recovery or an amount after any permitted costs. A bare check amount may leave out the details you need to understand the result.

Keep the deductible in its own line. If you also paid for a rental, tow, damaged property inside the car, or work the carrier did not approve, ask whether the company sought any of those amounts. The fact that it included a deductible does not show it included every out-of-pocket cost. Save receipts, payment confirmations and the original insurer’s explanation of which expenses were covered.

If the other driver offers to pay you directly, ask whether the offer comes with a release. An exchange of money and a signed document can affect more than the disputed $500 or $750. Review the proposed wording with your carrier if it is pursuing the property claim. If you also have injuries or unpaid bills, identify which of those claims would remain open. Put any confirmed reimbursement in your ledger only once.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I pay a deductible when I was not at fault?

You may owe one when using your own collision coverage. Ask whether the insurer expects to seek recovery from the other driver and whether a deductible waiver applies. If you file directly with the other insurer, its liability review is separate from the deductible on your policy.

### What if the covered repair costs less than the deductible?

A covered loss below the deductible generally produces no payment under that coverage. Check the full estimate before deciding that the claim is below the deductible. Damage that is hard to see can change the cost of repairs. Follow any policy notice requirements and legal reporting duties separately.

### Can my deductible be higher than the amount the shop wants up front?

Yes. The shop may collect payment at a different stage than the insurer issues its estimate. Ask who is expected to pay each part of the bill and when. A written breakdown helps avoid paying a charge that another party has already covered.

### Will GAP cover the deductible after a total loss?

Read the GAP agreement and ask for its calculation. Some agreements may address part of the deductible and others may exclude it. The [GAP insurance guide](/blogs/insurance-how-does-gap-insurance-work) explains how the deductible, loan payoff and market value fit together.
