## First, recognize when medical care cannot wait

After any potential head injury, safety comes before legal documentation. The CDC advises emergency evaluation for danger signs that can include worsening and persistent headache, repeated vomiting, seizures, unusual behavior, trouble recognizing people or places, slurred speech, marked drowsiness, weakness or unequal pupils. These symptoms can indicate a serious complication. Call 911 or seek emergency care rather than trying to preserve evidence while someone may be deteriorating.

A person can sustain a concussion from a blow to the head or from a jolt to the body that moves the head rapidly. Loss of consciousness is not required for a mild TBI. If symptoms such as headache, dizziness, confusion or difficulties concentrating emerge later, tell a medical professional both when the accident occurred and when the symptoms became apparent. Medical teams, not claim adjusters, decide what evaluation or imaging is appropriate.

Do not treat this guide as an assessment of any specific person. A legal record should faithfully describe what clinicians observed and what the injured person reported, without guessing at diagnoses or minimizing urgent symptoms.

## What the first 24 to 72 hours can establish

The earliest records may include emergency medical services notes, emergency-department history, observations by family or coworkers, the timing of symptoms, neurological examinations, medications, and discharge instructions. They can be useful for showing the sequence between an accident and later changes. They can also contain gaps: the person may have been dazed, distracted by another injury or unable to give a complete account.

When it is safe, preserve photographs of the scene, vehicle or helmet damage, time-stamped communications, witness contact information, insurance details and the incident report number. A passenger, cyclist or pedestrian should also preserve the exact location, road markings, signal conditions and the names of agencies that responded. Keep originals as well as copies, so dates and context remain clear.

A simple contemporaneous timeline is useful: date and time of the event; whether there was a head strike or abrupt acceleration; first symptoms; first clinical contact; follow-up appointments; and the first time someone noticed a meaningful change in memory, behavior or work. Record what is known, what is uncertain and who witnessed each fact.

## A normal CT scan does not answer every question

Imaging has a specific clinical purpose. The CDC explains that a brain scan is not required to diagnose every mild TBI; imaging may be used to evaluate the risk of bleeding or other acute structural problems. A normal scan therefore should not be portrayed as proof that no concussion occurred. Equally, symptoms alone do not establish that a particular collision legally caused all later problems.

Clinicians may evaluate orientation, attention, memory, balance, eye movements and other functions and decide whether specialist assessment is needed. Later neuropsychological or cognitive testing may help describe certain functional limitations. Interpret these findings with the relevant medical professionals; a blog cannot translate a test score into a diagnosis or settlement amount.

Where there is a disagreement about the nature or cause of an injury, an accurate sequence of examinations, symptom reports and treatment decisions is more useful than a conclusory statement that an MRI or CT 'proves' everything. Avoid making medical claims that the records do not support.

## Keep a functional diary without turning it into a performance

After the first evaluation, daily-life documentation can capture changes that are hard to see in appointment summaries. Brief notes might record a missed shift caused by fatigue, an inability to finish a normal work task, trouble following a conversation, a need for quiet breaks, difficulties driving safely, or extra help with household duties. Dates and specific examples are more meaningful than broad claims that every day is terrible.

Ask family members to retain their own observations in their own words. What changed about meal preparation, scheduling, school, irritability or sleep? A coworker may be able to describe reduced concentration or a change in duties. These accounts should be honest about good days and setbacks. Inaccurate or exaggerated journals can weaken rather than help the evaluation.

Protect privacy. Detailed medical records and family communications are sensitive; keep a secure copy and share only with appropriate clinicians or counsel. Social media posts rarely convey the clinical context of a fluctuating injury and should not be relied on as the main record.

## Differentiate a mild TBI from moderate or severe injury

A 'mild' TBI is a clinical classification, not a promise that a patient will have no meaningful difficulties. Many patients with concussion improve over days or weeks, while some need additional follow-up. CDC guidance notes that persistent or worsening symptoms should be discussed with a healthcare professional. The right response is not automatic prolonged bed rest: current CDC guidance describes brief initial rest followed by gradual return to activity as medically advised.

Moderate and severe TBIs can require a very different course of care, potentially including inpatient rehabilitation, speech-language therapy, physical and occupational therapy, counseling, memory strategies and help with basic tasks. Long-term needs vary substantially from person to person. Family members and providers may need to document how the person manages transportation, medications, work and personal independence.

Because outcomes differ, articles promising a universal recovery period or fixed settlement value are misleading. The actual medical trajectory and functional evidence should guide the claim.

## Accident reconstruction still matters

An injury record addresses the harm, not automatically the legal cause of the event. Separate the question 'What happened in the collision?' from 'What medical effects followed?' Traffic-signal timing, speed or turning movements, witness accounts, camera footage, the damage pattern and available vehicle data may help establish how an impact occurred. A fall may instead raise issues about a hazardous property condition and who had notice.

The responsible parties can also vary. An ordinary passenger-vehicle crash may involve a driver and vehicle owner; commercial vehicles can add an employer or carrier; a defective condition in a public roadway can create a different claims procedure. Preserve the information needed to identify those parties without assuming any person or company is responsible simply because a brain injury occurred.

Nearby video may be overwritten and physical evidence can change quickly. Ask an appropriate professional about preservation requests when footage, vehicles or company records may be involved.

## Measure work loss and future earning questions carefully

The economic picture is not just the wages missed immediately after the crash. A person may return on reduced hours, move to duties with fewer cognitive demands, lose overtime, or need retraining. Preserve pay stubs, attendance records, employer communications, tax returns where appropriate, and written work restrictions from treating providers.

A claim for future earning effects requires more than a guess about what might have happened. Work history, actual restrictions, educational background, vocational evidence and medical opinions may be relevant if a long-term limitation is supported. A self-employed person may need contracts, invoices and business records to distinguish lost opportunities from changes caused by other factors.

Do not assume that every episode of poor performance or stress was caused by the accident. An accurate assessment considers prior function, other health factors, the date changes appeared, and what the evidence can reasonably support.

## Future care: planning without claiming certainty

For more severe injuries, anticipated care can extend beyond hospital bills to specialist follow-up, rehabilitation, assistive technology, medication management, mobility or cognitive support, transportation, and changes to living arrangements. A life-care plan or other specialist evaluation may help organize future needs in some cases, but it should be grounded in individualized medical recommendations and realistic service costs.

A preliminary recommendation is not the same as a definite lifelong obligation. The record should distinguish treatment already received, care recommended now, care contingent on later evaluation, and needs that remain uncertain. Families should not be pressed to declare a permanent outcome before clinicians can reasonably assess it.

California civil-injury claims can include supported future problems and treatment needs; the California Courts self-help materials discuss medical bills, ongoing treatment, income and emotional harm as possible categories of losses. The precise legal standards and evidence should be reviewed by counsel.

## How insurance, fault and damages interact

A medically serious injury can coincide with disputed liability or limited insurance. Identifying available coverage may require checking another driver's liability policy, the vehicle owner, commercial or employment relationships, additional policies, and an injured person's own underinsured motorist coverage where applicable. That is separate from determining the total injury-related loss.

California comparative-fault principles can affect recovery where more than one party contributed to an accident. Medical severity does not itself resolve fault, and a single medical invoice does not define the value of lost daily function. Conversely, an early insurer valuation is not a final judicial determination of the injury's effects.

Do not accept a formula such as three times the medical bill as an official California rule. The best-supported evaluation starts with the evidence, applicable law and actual insurance circumstances.

## Know the deadlines before the prognosis is settled

California Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 generally provides two years for many actions involving personal injury caused by another's wrongful act or neglect. But the correct deadline can differ by defendant or legal theory, and a public entity can trigger an administrative government-claim requirement substantially earlier. California Government Code section 911.2 generally provides a six-month presentment period for injury-related government claims, subject to applicable exceptions and procedures.

A patient does not need to reach 'maximum medical improvement' before seeking deadline advice. Waiting for a full long-term prognosis can be dangerous if witness memories, video or a legal filing period are disappearing in parallel. Insurance-policy notice provisions and preservation obligations may create additional questions that are not answered by the general two-year rule.

Keep litigation deadlines and medical appointments as separate items on your timeline. Neither insurer negotiations nor ongoing rehabilitation automatically extend the limitations period.

## Questions to ask at the first lawyer consultation

Bring the collision or incident details, available photos and reports, insurance letters, the names of every treating facility, known work restrictions and a short timeline of symptoms. Ask which evidence may be lost, which insurers or other parties should be considered, what legal deadlines apply, and how later medical developments should be recorded. A candid explanation of any prior conditions helps counsel evaluate causation responsibly.

Ask how the firm handles communication with healthcare providers, whether it has appropriate expert resources for the medical issues, how case costs and contingency fees are set out in the written agreement, and how settlement decisions are explained. No lawyer can responsibly guarantee a brain-injury outcome or dollar figure from a short intake call.
