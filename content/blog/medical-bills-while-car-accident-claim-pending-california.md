You do not need to settle an injury claim simply because the first bill arrives. You do need a plan for that bill. Ask the billing office what it has submitted, which insurer has responded, and what it currently expects from you. A letter from the other driver’s insurer acknowledging a claim is different from an agreement by your provider to wait for payment.

This guide focuses on the work to do while a claim is pending. For immediate scene records and reporting steps, begin with the [California crash guide](/blogs/what-to-do-after-a-car-accident-in-california).

## Separate the treatment account from the compensation claim

The provider’s account concerns particular services and payments. An injury claim may concern responsibility, medical evidence, lost income and other supported losses. Those questions overlap, but they are not resolved by the same document. A provider statement does not establish fault, and an adjuster’s investigation does not show whether your health plan processed a visit.

Make a list of the organizations involved. The hospital, emergency physician, imaging service and ambulance provider may have separate billing contacts. Record the service date and account reference for each. Ask whether a statement is a new bill, a corrected bill or a reminder for an existing account before entering it as another expense.

Do not assume your lawyer pays ongoing treatment charges. Ask what the representation agreement says and who is responsible for current accounts. Likewise, an insurer’s willingness to discuss a future settlement does not establish a payment arrangement with a medical provider.

## Check which coverage may address a bill now

Your health plan may provide a route for covered treatment, subject to its terms. Give the provider accurate plan information and ask the plan about referrals, network rules and any accident questionnaire it requires. Keep copies of requests and responses rather than assuming one insurer will obtain everything from another.

Optional medical payments coverage, often called MedPay, may also be relevant under an auto policy. California’s insurance department explains that this coverage can address limited medical expenses regardless of fault. Check whether it was purchased, who is insured, the available limit and the submission requirements. It is not a substitute for checking the actual policy. [California Department of Insurance auto coverage guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/auto101.cfm)

A claim involving an uninsured driver, a rideshare trip or a vehicle used for work can raise additional questions. Identify that circumstance early instead of assigning the bill to a policy from its name alone. Our [uninsured-driver guide](/blogs/california-uninsured-driver-hit-and-run-injury-claims) and [rideshare insurance guide](/blogs/uber-lyft-accident-insurance-california) explain the related claim issues.

## Read the explanation of benefits beside the provider statement

A health plan’s explanation of benefits, or EOB, is not itself a bill. It explains how the plan processed a service. Compare it with the provider’s current statement before deciding what remains to be resolved. [CMS guidance on reading an EOB](https://www.cms.gov/initiatives/your-patient-rights/medical-bill-rights/get-help/guides-resources/read-explanation-benefits)

Check the patient, provider, date of service and claim reference first. Then identify the charge, plan payment, adjustment and any stated patient responsibility. If the insurer says a claim is awaiting information, ask exactly what is missing and who must supply it. A request for an accident date calls for a different response from a denial based on a plan exclusion.

Keep earlier versions when a statement changes. A new balance may reflect a payment being posted, a correction or additional services. Ask for the account history if you cannot reconcile it. Record the explanation next to the bill so you do not have to reconstruct the same conversation later.

## Use a ledger that shows what happened to each charge

A list of initial charges is useful, but it is not a reliable list of current unpaid balances. Keep separate columns for money paid and adjustments. Do not add an EOB total to a provider bill as though they were two different treatment expenses.

| Entry | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Service | Provider, visit date and account reference |
| Initial charge | Amount shown on the original itemized statement |
| Plan processing | Payment, adjustment, denial or request for information |
| Other payment | MedPay or another payer’s amount and recipient |
| Your payment | Amount, date and receipt |
| Current balance | Latest provider statement and date confirmed |
| Next step | Specific missing document, contact and follow-up date |

**Hypothetical example.** A clinic bills $600. Your health plan's EOB shows a $200 adjustment, a $300 plan payment and $100 in patient responsibility. You already paid the clinic $40 at the visit. Ask whether the clinic applied that payment to the same account before paying the next statement. If it did, the remaining balance in this example would be $60. Keep the EOB and the receipt with the original bill. The ledger tracks what is owed to the clinic. The value of the injury claim depends on additional evidence.

## Ask about payment arrangements before assuming an account can wait

If you cannot pay the stated balance, contact the billing office rather than relying on an expected settlement date. Ask whether it offers financial assistance, a payment plan or a temporary review hold. Request the terms in writing, including the amount, due dates and what happens when the review ends. The office may not agree to the arrangement you request.

Be specific about the account involved. An agreement with a hospital may not cover a separate physician or ambulance bill. Ask whether each organization has its own application and contact. Retain the application, documents submitted and any decision so you can follow up on the actual request.

California hospitals must maintain financial-assistance policies for qualifying patients. HCAI’s Hospital Bill Complaint Program explains its role in complaints about those policies and certain collection practices. It does not handle every general billing dispute or every separately billed professional service. [HCAI hospital bill complaint guidance](https://hcai.ca.gov/affordability/hospital-fair-billing-program/hospital-bill-complaint-program/)

## Understand a proposed medical lien before signing

A provider may propose an agreement involving payment from a later recovery. Read the actual terms rather than treating the word “lien” as a promise that treatment is free. Ask how charges are calculated, whether insurance will be billed, and what you could owe if the claim produces less money than expected or no recovery.

Ask for a copy before agreeing. Identify which provider, services and dates it covers and whether other offices would require separate agreements. Compare it with available insurance and financial-assistance options. Have the legal consequences reviewed if the language or potential obligation is unclear.

Do not assume every amount requested will be reduced at settlement. A negotiation may be possible, but the right to reimbursement and any reduction depend on the arrangement and applicable law. Keep a current list of claimed obligations while the case develops, rather than discovering them only when a release arrives.

## Account for public-benefit and other reimbursement questions

Medi-Cal may have reimbursement rights when it paid injury-related services and the member receives a qualifying recovery. DHCS operates a Personal Injury Program for that process. Tell counsel if Medi-Cal paid for relevant treatment and retain the program’s correspondence. [DHCS Personal Injury Program](https://www.dhcs.ca.gov/services/personal-injury-program/)

Other plans and benefit programs can have their own requirements. Keep the plan name, payment history and reimbursement notices available for review. A balance owed to the provider and an amount claimed by a payer are different entries; identify both before assuming the remaining settlement belongs entirely to you.

Ask for a written explanation of proposed deductions and unresolved claims before approving a distribution. A settlement’s headline amount does not show what remains after fees, case expenses and valid obligations. The [California case-value guide](/blogs/how-much-is-my-personal-injury-case-worth-california) explains why the final assessment extends beyond adding bills.

## Bring a focused billing packet to a consultation

Start with a short treatment timeline, current provider statements, EOBs, proof of your payments and any coverage or reimbursement letters. Include the insurer’s proposed release if one exists. Mark missing records and immediate due dates on the first page. For a claim involving a vehicle collision, use the [car accident claim overview](/practice-areas/car-accidents) to see where billing records fit alongside the evidence about fault and lost income.

Keep sensitive records in a private folder. Ask the office how to deliver them securely; an initial website message can describe the issue without uploading an entire medical history. A first conversation does not itself transfer responsibility for a deadline or establish representation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will the other driver’s insurer pay every bill as it arrives?

Do not assume so. Ask the adjuster what payment is being considered and whether it would require a release. Separately ask the provider about the current account. A pending liability investigation does not establish that the provider agreed to postpone payment.

### Should I ignore a bill while my lawyer negotiates?

No. Send the relevant notice to your lawyer if represented, verify the balance with the provider and ask what response is needed. Keep the actual notice and deadline. Do not rely on a general statement that the case is still open.

### Does MedPay mean I cannot pursue an injury claim?

A first-party medical benefit and a liability claim address different questions. The effect of payments and any reimbursement provision needs review under the policy and applicable law. Keep payment records so each amount can be accounted for.

### What if the health plan denies treatment charges?

Obtain the stated reason, applicable plan terms and review instructions. Check whether the denial concerns missing information, coverage or another issue. For plans regulated by DMHC, its [complaint instructions](https://www.dmhc.ca.gov/FileaComplaint.aspx) explain the available process; different coverage may have a different regulator or appeal route.

### Does hiring a lawyer erase my medical debt?

No. Representation does not itself cancel bills or guarantee that a provider will wait, accept a lien or reduce a balance. Ask what assistance the office can provide and what obligations remain yours.

### Can I finish a settlement before every billing question is resolved?

That requires individual advice. Identify outstanding treatment needs, current balances and reimbursement claims before deciding what a release and distribution would mean. Do not treat an estimated net amount as a final accounting.
