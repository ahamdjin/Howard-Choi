## What should I save from the rental app or scooter?

After addressing immediate safety and medical needs, capture the ride receipt, trip ID, time and map from your account. Save the scooter’s QR label or serial number if you can do so safely. Keep messages to and from the rental company, including any case number. A trip ending on the map does not necessarily establish where the impact occurred, so write down the nearest address, cross street and direction of travel separately.

For a privately owned scooter, keep the make, model, photographs, purchase information and any maintenance history. If you noticed a brake or steering problem before the crash, write down what you experienced. Preserve the scooter’s condition and avoid attempting to reproduce the failure.

## Which photos and witness details should I get first?

A turning-car collision and a fall after hitting a pavement defect need different evidence. For the first, document the driveway, turning path, signals, paint and sight lines. For the second, capture the defect, its location relative to the curb or lane, and wider photographs that help show visibility. Take photos only from a safe place. Nearby witnesses or businesses may have video. Note the camera location and the time range so an owner can find the right recording.

The [California right-of-way guide](/blogs/right-of-way-accident-claims-california) explains how road layout and movements matter when accounts conflict. If a roadway condition might involve a government entity, check the [California Courts guide to government claims](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/government-claim) promptly because a different claims process and shorter deadlines may apply.

## Which California scooter rules might matter after a crash?

California distinguishes a motorized scooter from an e-bike, moped or seated motorcycle. The [CHP traffic enforcement manual](https://www.chp.ca.gov/siteassets/policy/hpm/hpm-100.68/hpm-100.68-ch-4_sanitized.pdf) explains how Vehicle Code sections 407.5, 21235 and 22411 apply to stand-up motorized scooters. The operator needs a valid driver’s license or instruction permit. Riders under 18 must wear a properly fitted, fastened bicycle helmet, and motorized scooters may not carry passengers. The [moped and scooter comparison](/blogs/mopeds-vs-scooters-california) explains the differences among vehicle classes.

The scooter operating limit is 15 mph, including in bikeways. That limit is separate from the posted road limit. On a highway posted above 25 mph, section 21235(b) generally requires a Class II bike lane or Class IV separated bikeway. A local ordinance or resolution can permit travel outside those bikeways on roads posted at up to 35 mph. Section 21235(g) permits sidewalk operation only as needed to enter or leave adjacent property. If the crash occurred near a driveway, record whether you were moving along the sidewalk, crossing it to enter or leave adjacent property, or traveling in a bike lane. Save photos of both the posted limit and the bikeway marking if either may be disputed.

## Whose insurer or rental company should I contact?

If a car was involved, keep the driver’s insurance information and any police report number. If it was a rental scooter, save the rental agreement in effect for your trip and ask the operator about available coverage in writing. If a delivery or other work activity was involved, preserve the assignment and timing records. Coverage depends on the parties, facts and policy language. The [third-party auto insurance claim guide](/blogs/third-party-car-insurance-claim-california) explains what to document when making a claim with another driver’s insurer.

Keep equipment losses and medical records in separate folders. Photograph the scooter and other damaged property, and retain treatment bills and missed-work records. If someone offers to reimburse a specific item, read what the payment would settle before signing a release.

## What if the app shows a different crash time or location?

Write down when you unlocked the scooter, when you remember the crash happening, when you ended the ride and when you sought help. Record the time zone if an app export uses a different clock. If you spoke to a dispatcher and the trip remained open while you waited, keep both records. The emergency call and trip end may be minutes apart because they capture different events.

Use an exact address or map pin for the impact, and describe it in plain language. A scooter trip might end near the corner of an intersection while the crash happened midway across a driveway half a block earlier. A note such as “at the second driveway north of the bus stop, traveling toward the intersection” helps someone locate the event without assuming the GPS marker is exact. Keep an unedited copy of any map or receipt. Mark a separate copy when you want to point out where you believe the impact occurred.

## What evidence helps if a driver crossed my path?

Record the direction of travel and the point at which the driver entered the crossing, driveway or lane. If other traffic blocked someone’s view, note the lane and the type of vehicle involved. Photograph the lane markings, signs and any surrounding features that help explain sight distance. Avoid stepping into traffic to recreate the view. A photograph from a nearby safe position, with a note about where it was taken, is useful even if it does not reproduce the rider’s precise angle.

The driver’s insurance card and plate number help identify the people and vehicles involved. Ask witnesses for their contact details and where they were standing. A person on the same corner might see the light change, while a driver behind the scooter might have a dashcam view of the car turning. Keep any full clips that witnesses share, including the lead-up to the collision.

## What if the scooter’s brakes or steering failed?

Describe what you felt, heard or saw and when it started. Did the handlebars pull after the front wheel hit a seam? Did the brake lever feel loose before you reached the curb? Note any message on the display and any prior problem reported during the trip. These observations help distinguish between a possible equipment issue, a road feature and another cause.

Check the exact model and serial number against the [U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission’s e-scooter recall listings](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Micromobility-Information-Center). The [CPSC’s Razor Icon recall](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2024/Razor-Recalls-Icon-Electric-Scooters-Due-to-Fall-Hazard), for example, identifies affected models and serial numbers and describes how separation at the downtube could cause a fall. A recall can identify what an inspection should examine. Whether the same failure happened on your ride depends on the condition of your scooter and the crash evidence.

For a rental, record the device ID and contact the operator through its support channel. Save the case number and ask whether it can preserve trip records and identify what happened to the specific unit after the report. A rental fleet may move a unit from the street, so promptly recording the ID matters. For a personally owned scooter, keep repair receipts, parts information and photos showing its condition. Get advice before allowing an inspection that would involve disassembly, repair or disposal if the equipment may be important to a claim.

## What if I fell because of a pothole or work zone?

Take one wider picture showing the full approach and another showing the hazard. If you can safely do so, record a fixed reference such as a street number, a signpost or a marked driveway. Write down when you took the photos. A photo from after a repair, a change in lighting or removal of a barricade should be marked with its actual date. It can still help orient someone without representing how the site looked at the time.

Note any temporary markings, construction signs or contractor names visible from a safe location. A city street can cross a state route, and the agency that responds to a crash may differ from the entity responsible for a specific part of the road. If the condition may implicate a public agency, find out promptly which agency to contact and which claim process applies. A maintenance complaint or a report number does not by itself establish that the formal claim was filed. The [California Courts government claim guide](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/government-claim) says an injury claim generally must be submitted to the appropriate public entity within six months, with exceptions. If the City of Buena Park is the relevant agency, its [City Clerk claim form](https://cms7files1.revize.com/buenaparkca/Document_center/City%20Departments/City%20clerk/CLAIMFORMFillable.pdf) shows the location, dates and other details it asks for. Confirm the entity responsible for the specific stretch of road before using a city form.

## Do I need a police report or a DMV filing?

If an officer responded, record the agency name and incident number. Ask how to get the report and when it will be available. A report can help identify parties, but it may not include information you later get from a business camera or a witness. Keep those records alongside it. If a motor vehicle was involved, check California’s separate [DMV accident reporting rules](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/dmv-virtual-office/accident-reporting/). For drivers required to file an SR-1, DMV lists a 10-day deadline when someone is injured or killed or when property damage exceeds $1,000. Verify who must file in your situation, especially if the event involved a scooter alone. A scooter rental support ticket, a police report and a DMV filing each serve different purposes.

Keep a record of what each insurer or company requested, when you responded and any stated deadline. If you receive conflicting explanations, ask which coverage, agreement or facts each party is referring to. The [California Department of Insurance’s accident claims guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/hadaccident.cfm) explains the standard auto claims process when an auto policy is involved. Coverage for the scooter or rider may raise additional questions.

## An example of a useful first evidence packet

Imagine a rider in Buena Park says a car pulled across a bike lane to enter a parking lot. The driver says the rider was on the sidewalk. The rental receipt confirms the time range, but the map only shows the trip ending near the entrance. A photo of the driveway and curb cut can locate the crossing. A nearby business camera and a witness at the storefront may help establish where the scooter was before the driver turned. The report number provides a way to request the investigating officer’s record.

Put the receipt, scooter ID, location note, photos and witness contacts in one folder. List the open questions separately, such as whether the storefront has footage or which insurer handles the driver’s claim. If you are also receiving treatment, keep the appointment dates, bills and work notes in a second folder. That makes it easier to discuss the immediate evidence while the injury records are still developing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I do if the rental app no longer shows the ride?

Look for a receipt or confirmation in your email, text messages and payment history. Note the time, account, and payment reference before you contact the operator. Ask for the ride history and any incident reference tied to that trip. Explain clearly which details you can confirm and which you are estimating.

### Can I use a screenshot if I cannot download a file?

Yes, a screenshot can preserve information you can see. Capture the date, trip ID and surrounding context when available, and keep the original screenshot separate from cropped or annotated copies. If the operator later supplies an export, save it as well and note when you received it.
