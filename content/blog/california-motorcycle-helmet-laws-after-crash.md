## Do passengers have to wear a motorcycle helmet?

Yes, when riding on California highways. The [California DMV Motorcycle Handbook](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/file/motorcycle-driver-handbook-pdf/) explains that Vehicle Code section 27803 applies to both operators and passengers on motorcycles, motor-driven cycles and motorized bicycles. Under section 27803(e), the helmet must sit securely on the person’s head without excessive movement, with its straps fastened. The [California Attorney General’s opinion on section 27803](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/opinions/pdfs/98-1003.pdf) reproduces that requirement. Section 27803(f) has a narrow exception for certain fully enclosed three-wheel vehicles. A regular motorcycle passenger should expect the same helmet requirement as the operator. A passenger’s obligation is separate from any restrictions on carrying passengers under a motorcycle permit. See the [California motorcycle license and permit guide](/blogs/california-motorcycle-license-permit-requirements) for that distinction.

## Can helmet use affect a California injury claim?

An insurer may argue that a helmet issue contributed to particular injuries. The questions are what the evidence shows about the helmet and whether there is a connection to the injury being claimed. An injury mechanism may need medical or other expert evaluation. Preserve the original account and records before trying to infer what happened from damage to the helmet alone.

See the [California comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) for how disputed responsibility is evaluated. The [brain injury evidence guide](/blogs/traumatic-brain-injury-evidence-after-california-accident) covers early symptoms, treatment records and observations from people who knew the rider before the collision.

## What does the DOT label show?

For a helmet manufactured on or after May 13, 2013, [FMVSS No. 218](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-49/subtitle-B/chapter-V/part-571/subpart-B/section-571.218) specifies a rear certification label identifying the manufacturer or brand, model, DOT, the standard number and certification. The same federal standard sets performance requirements for impact attenuation, penetration resistance and the retention system that holds the helmet in place. [NHTSA’s helmet guide](https://www.nhtsa.gov/motorcycle-safety/choose-right-motorcycle-helmet) explains what the marking means, why labels on older helmets differ and how to check fit. The label reflects manufacturer certification. NHTSA does not individually approve each helmet model before it is sold, and counterfeit labels exist.

If you still have the packaging, receipt or product listing, keep it with your records. Those documents can help identify the model when the label is scratched or partly missing. With the exact brand and model in hand, check [NHTSA’s equipment recall database](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and compare the notice with the size, manufacture date and other identifiers on the helmet. A recall may apply to only some units. A matching notice is a reason to preserve the equipment and have its condition evaluated alongside the crash records.

## What should I save after a motorcycle crash?

Once immediate medical and safety needs are addressed, photograph the helmet from several angles. Include the strap, buckle, label, visible marks and any areas damaged or cut during rescue. Write down who collected it and when. Preserve its condition and seek advice before discarding it or surrendering it for an inspection.

The helmet is one item in a larger record. Save the report number, witness names, video and photos showing lane positions or impact points. The [California motorcycle accident guide](/blogs/california-motorcycle-accident-guide) explains how these facts fit together. If a driver turned across the motorcycle’s path, evidence of the turn and the traffic controls can still matter even when the parties disagree about helmet use.

## Does the same helmet rule apply to mopeds and stand-up scooters?

California applies the motorcycle helmet rule to mopeds classified as motorized bicycles. Stand-up motorized scooters have a separate rule that requires a bicycle helmet for operators under 18. If a rental listing or crash report just says “scooter,” identify the vehicle before deciding which rule applies. Save a photo of the vehicle and write down the make and model. If the registration or manufacturer paperwork is available, keep it. That information is more useful than the name on a rental listing or the word a witness used in a report.

The [California DMV’s vehicle classification guide](https://www.dmv.ca.gov/portal/vehicle-registration/new-registration/motorcycles-mopeds-and-scooters/) explains the differences. If there were two people on a motorcycle, identify who was operating it and who was riding as a passenger. Keep the two helmets and their records separate even if both came from the same manufacturer. Distinguishing the equipment can matter if a report lists one helmet or a photograph shows both riders’ gear together.

## What if the helmet came off or the strap was cut?

Record when the helmet was last seen on the rider, where it was found and who handled it after the crash. A helmet lying in the road may draw immediate attention, but that location alone does not establish whether the strap was fastened before the collision. A witness who saw the rider leave a parking lot may know something different from a person who arrived after the impact. Ask each person to describe what they actually saw and when.

If a strap was cut, ask whether an emergency responder removed the helmet as part of care. The responding crew’s records or a witness account may help establish the sequence. Photograph the strap and buckle as they are. Keep loose pieces with the helmet, and record who recovered them. Avoid fastening a broken buckle or trying to recreate what happened using the helmet. The item is more useful for an inspection in the condition in which it was recovered.

## What if I can’t find the helmet after the crash?

Check with the investigating agency, the tow company and anyone who collected the rider’s belongings. Ask for a property receipt or inventory if one exists. If family members have started making calls, keep one shared note of whom they contacted and what they were told. A clear record saves time if an insurer asks where the equipment is.

If the helmet cannot be located, look for records made before the crash. A clear photo of the rider wearing it may show the helmet’s shape, color or markings. A receipt may link it to a model or order number. If you were wearing a helmet-mounted camera, preserve the original files and note whether its clock was accurate. A short clip attached to a message can help identify the event, while the original recording may show what happened in the seconds before and after it.

## What if the police report gets the helmet details wrong?

An officer can record what people at the scene said, along with the officer’s own observations. Find the passage about the helmet and see whether it identifies the source of the information. A report prepared before the helmet was located may say less than the investigation ultimately established. Likewise, a photograph with no timestamp or context may be difficult to place in the timeline.

Keep your original copy of the report and note the exact page or line you want to clarify. Collect the photographs, records or witness details that bear on that point. If you contact the investigating agency, ask how it handles supplemental information. The [guide to correcting an inaccurate California crash report](/blogs/correct-inaccurate-car-accident-police-report-california) explains what to organize before you make the request.

## An example of why the order of events matters

Suppose a driver turns left across the path of a motorcycle and the passenger is thrown to the pavement. The driver says the motorcycle was traveling too fast. The insurer later asks why the passenger’s helmet was found next to the bike. These questions call for different records. Video from across the intersection may show the turning movement and when the motorcycle entered. A witness standing near the passenger may know when the helmet came off. The paramedic report and photographs may show what happened after the passenger landed.

For the injury portion of the claim, the medical record and the helmet’s condition may help an expert evaluate the mechanism of a specific injury. They cannot substitute for evidence of the driver’s turn or establish the motorcycle’s speed by themselves. The [California right-of-way guide](/blogs/right-of-way-accident-claims-california) explains what to look for when parties give different accounts of an intersection crash.

## What should I ask if the insurer blames my helmet?

Ask the adjuster to identify the conduct at issue, the evidence behind it and the injuries the insurer says it affected. A general statement about the helmet leaves several questions open. For example, whether a rider wore a compliant helmet and whether a particular injury would have been different are separate factual issues. California’s [2026 civil jury instructions on comparative fault, CACI No. 405](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf), put the burden on the defendant to prove both that the injured person was negligent and that this negligence was a substantial factor in causing the harm. Applied to a helmet dispute, that means identifying the alleged conduct and the particular harm it is claimed to have caused or worsened. A helmet allegation provides no automatic percentage reduction.

Save the original letter or email. If the subject comes up on a call, record the date, the adjuster’s name and what evidence they requested. If the insurer proposes an inspection, ask how it will document the helmet on receipt and whether any testing could change its condition. Discuss the scope and handling before shipping the only original item.

## What information should I bring to a consultation?

Make a one-page timeline with the crash date, location, the first medical visit and the location of the motorcycle and helmet. Add the report number and insurer’s contact information if you have them. List any gaps you want help with, such as a missing camera file, an unanswered question in the report or uncertainty about who has the helmet. Keep original files separately from edited images or notes.

If you were the passenger, include your own account of where you were seated and what you could see. If you are helping a family member, record who has firsthand information and the best way to reach them. For local help organizing those records, see the [Buena Park motorcycle accident practice page](/practice-areas/motorcycle-accidents).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a DOT sticker on its own enough to identify the helmet?

A photo of the sticker is a starting point. Save any other manufacturer markings, model details and purchase records so the helmet can be identified as a whole. A fuzzy image of the sticker may be less useful than a clear photograph of the entire label and the inside markings.

### What if the helmet looks untouched?

Keep it and photograph its current condition. An undamaged-looking shell does not establish what the rider experienced, and a visible scrape does not establish when it happened. For a claim review, the equipment record and the medical timeline serve different purposes. For future use, follow the manufacturer’s advice about a helmet that may have taken an impact.
