## What an adjuster actually decides

You can hear from several adjusters after one collision. Your own carrier may assign someone to inspect the car under collision coverage. The other driver’s insurer may assign another person to decide whether it will accept responsibility and how much it is willing to pay. A bodily injury adjuster may enter the picture after the vehicle estimate is already finished. The repair shop may also have an estimator. Write down each person’s employer, assignment, and claim number so their decisions do not get mixed up.

An adjuster typically gathers accounts of the crash, photographs, statements, repair estimates, and other records. The adjuster may compare the documents with the policy, request a further inspection, or recommend a payment. Ask what has been decided and what is still pending. If the driver was using someone else’s vehicle, start by checking [whether coverage follows the car or the driver](/blogs/does-car-insurance-follow-the-car-or-driver-california). “We have coverage” might mean the policy was active, while the company is still investigating whether the driver was insured for this trip or who caused the impact. [California Department of Insurance’s accident guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/01-auto/hadaccident.cfm)

If the insurer assigns fault based on one version of events, ask which records it relied on. The [California comparative fault guide](/blogs/california-comparative-fault-personal-injury) explains how conflicting evidence can affect a claim. You can disagree with an assessment and still cooperate with a reasonable request for documents.

## Use the first call to identify the request

Before sharing sensitive information, verify the caller’s identity through the insurer’s published contact number or your existing claim portal. Ask whether the call is about damage to the vehicle, an injury, both, or the coverage for another person. Then ask whether the insurer is recording the call and whether it is requesting a formal statement.

Your own policy may require prompt notice and cooperation. Ask which provision applies if your insurer requests an interview, examination, or particular document. An adjuster from the other driver’s carrier is asking you for information on that carrier’s behalf. Ask for the request in writing if you need to see its scope before answering. Treat these conversations differently and follow the obligations in your own policy.

Early questions often sound simple. “Were you hurt?” can become difficult to answer accurately while symptoms are still being evaluated. Say what you know at the time. If you went to urgent care and have a follow-up appointment, say that. Avoid guessing how long recovery will take or offering a diagnosis that nobody has made. If you cannot remember the precise time or speed, check your records and provide an accurate correction later.

## What a written request lets you check

A request for a photo of the damaged fender is different from a medical authorization with an open-ended date range. Before signing a release of information, check the provider names, dates, records requested, permitted recipients, and expiration. Ask why each category is relevant. If the request is broad, ask whether the adjuster will review the relevant records or bills you submit directly. Keep a copy of any form you sign.

For a vehicle inspection, ask whether the estimate includes hidden damage found after disassembly. A repair shop may find a damaged bracket or sensor after taking off the bumper. Ask the carrier to separate any unapproved repair charges from [your collision deductible](/blogs/car-insurance-deductible-after-accident-california). Ask who sends the supplement, whether the carrier must approve it before work proceeds, and how delays affect rental coverage or storage fees. Compare the written estimates line by line. A difference may come from labor, parts, calibration, or a missing repair rather than a disagreement about the entire claim.

For an injury review, separate bills from records. A bill shows what was charged. A treatment note may show symptoms, restrictions, or a clinician’s findings. A wage statement documents a different loss. An organized file lets the adjuster identify what is still missing without requiring you to send the same packet again.

## A practical example of one crash and two files

A driver is rear-ended on the way to work. The first adjuster approves bumper repairs. A second adjuster requests information about the driver’s missed shifts. The driver sends everything to the first adjuster because that is the only claim number they have written down. Two weeks later the injury adjuster says they are still waiting for the employer letter.

The next step is to confirm who received which document and resend only what the injury adjuster needs. Put the correct claim number in the subject line. Ask for confirmation of receipt and for a specific list of remaining items. Separately, ask the vehicle adjuster whether the repair payment requires a release. If it does, obtain a copy and check whether the language is limited to the car or also reaches injury claims.

That distinction matters even when both adjusters work for the same company. The car can be fixed while treatment continues, and accepting a repair payment may have different consequences from signing a broad settlement agreement. The [California settlement timeline guide](/blogs/how-long-car-accident-settlement-takes-california) explains how these parts of a claim can move at different speeds.

## Build a claim log that helps resolve disputes

- Record when you reported the crash, the claim numbers, and which adjuster handles each part.
- Keep each estimate with its issue date, the shop that prepared it, and any later supplements.
- File photographs in original form, along with copies of what you uploaded to the portal.
- Write down what you sent, when the insurer received it, and what the adjuster said would happen next.
- Save each coverage or fault decision as a separate document. Ask for the policy provision and facts relied on when the decision is unclear.
- If you correct something you previously said, record when and how you sent the correction.

After an important call, a short written follow-up helps. State your understanding of what was requested, what was supplied, and what remains outstanding. If the insurer says it cannot reach a decision yet, ask what it is investigating and when you should expect the next update. California’s claims regulations set standards for communications and decisions, with different obligations depending on the stage of the claim. [California fair claims regulations](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/130-laws-regs-hearings/05-CCR/fair-claims-regs.cfm)

If injuries or fault remain in dispute, bring the claim log and the adjuster’s letters when asking a [car accident lawyer](/practice-areas/car-accidents) about your options.

## If the insurer is waiting for another record

Ask for the exact record and why it matters. If the adjuster says it is waiting for a medical bill, find out whether the missing item is the amount charged, the treatment note, or proof that the account remains unpaid. Calling the provider for an itemized bill can resolve one problem. An open request for records from several unrelated providers can raise a different issue that needs a clear explanation.

When the insurer is waiting for its own insured’s statement, you may have little control over that interview. Offer other available evidence and ask when the adjuster will review it. If the company has received a camera recording but still says it cannot establish what happened, ask whether it has watched the full clip and whether a particular part is unclear. Send the timing and location information necessary to interpret the clip.

After you supply a record, avoid guessing what happens next. Ask whether it satisfies the request, whether another decision maker must review it, and what you should expect to hear back. A single email with the file number and a list of attachments is easier to locate than several messages with different subject lines. If a request involves a deadline, confirm the date independently and keep proof of delivery.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the adjuster’s first offer final?

An offer can change as additional evidence is reviewed. Ask how it was calculated, which losses it includes, and whether it would require a release. If you have a missing estimate, medical bill, or wage record, identify the specific item before responding.

### Should I give a recorded statement to the other insurer?

Find out whose insurer is asking and what it wants to establish. Your obligations to your own carrier and a request from another driver’s carrier differ. Ask about the purpose and scope of the statement before agreeing, and correct any factual errors in writing as soon as you find them.

### Can I ask to see why an insurer denied part of the claim?

Yes. Request a written explanation that identifies the claim, the part denied, and the reason for the decision. If the disagreement concerns a policy term, ask for the text of the provision. If it concerns facts, find out which records the adjuster used and which documents it has yet to review.

### What if the adjuster changes mid-claim?

Ask the new adjuster to confirm which documents carried over. Resend the current estimate or other important records if the file is incomplete. Keep the previous correspondence so you can identify any commitments, requests, or inspection dates that still need a response.
