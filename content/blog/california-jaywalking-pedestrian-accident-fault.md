## What changed under California’s Freedom to Walk Act?

California’s Freedom to Walk Act limits when an officer may stop someone for certain pedestrian crossing infractions. The threshold concerns whether a reasonably careful person would recognize an immediate danger of collision with a moving vehicle or a device propelled exclusively by human power. The provision addresses the police stop. A person injured while crossing still faces a separate question about how the collision happened and which actions contributed to it. [San Diego Police Department bulletin on the Freedom to Walk Act](https://www.sandiego.gov/sites/default/files/tb_22-17_revised_freedom_to_walk_act_updates_to_vehicle_code.pdf)

That distinction matters in an ordinary conversation after a crash. A driver might tell an insurer, “They were jaywalking.” A pedestrian might respond, “The officer never gave me a ticket.” Neither statement tells you where the pedestrian entered the street, whether the driver was turning, what each could see or when either had time to react. Start with those facts before attaching a label to the crossing.

## Was it a marked crosswalk, an unmarked crosswalk or a midblock crossing?

People often use “crosswalk” to mean white stripes on the pavement. California also recognizes unmarked crosswalks at qualifying intersections. A person crossing from one corner to the opposite corner may be in an unmarked crosswalk. An opening between parked cars halfway down a block is a different setting. If you are uncertain where the crossing began, identify the curb, nearest intersection, and direction of travel rather than guessing at the legal description.

Look for features that help locate the crossing later. A driveway, curb ramp, shop entrance, pedestrian signal or median may appear in a photo or on a map. Note whether the person started on the corner and walked straight across, moved diagonally toward another corner, or entered the roadway farther down the block. A pin dropped after an ambulance arrived may mark the ambulance location rather than where the person stepped off the curb.

At a marked or unmarked crosswalk, drivers generally must yield to a crossing pedestrian. Pedestrians also must use care when stepping into traffic. Outside a crosswalk, a pedestrian generally must yield to vehicles close enough to create an immediate hazard. Drivers still have a duty to exercise due care for pedestrian safety. Traffic controls and the exact crossing location can change which provisions apply. [California Vehicle Code provisions governing pedestrians](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?division=11.&chapter=5.&lawCode=VEH)

## Build the sequence from both sides of the street

Consider a hypothetical crash near a row of shops. A pedestrian steps out between two parked cars. The driver says the pedestrian appeared suddenly. A witness says the person had already crossed a full lane before impact. Both details deserve investigation. The parked cars may have obscured the first step, while the time spent in the open lane may bear on what happened afterward. Establish which direction the witness faced and which lane they could see.

Separate the moment the person entered the roadway from the moment they entered the vehicle’s path. Mark any pause at a median, a change in walking direction or a turn by the vehicle. Someone who was standing in a center refuge before a driver turned may have been visible from a different angle than someone who just emerged from behind a bus. These distinctions can disappear when an entire crossing is summarized in a single sentence.

In a signalized intersection, identify both the pedestrian display and the vehicle movement. A person who begins crossing on a walk signal may still be in the crosswalk when the display changes. A witness who saw a flashing hand only after hearing the collision may know little about the display at the start. Record what each person saw and when. A later photo of the signal documents its location and layout, but it cannot replay the earlier cycle.

## What sight lines and lighting can explain

A driver turning into a side street may be looking toward approaching traffic before looking into the turn. A delivery vehicle can block part of the sidewalk from that position. A pedestrian may be watching one lane while another vehicle turns across their route. Draw the direction of those movements, then mark what each person says first drew their attention. A simple sketch often exposes a missing detail more clearly than a long account.

If the crash happened at dusk, record the approximate time, weather and direction of travel. Identify nearby streetlights, illuminated signs and shadows, but avoid estimating how far a person could see unless you have a basis for it. A daytime street image cannot reproduce glare from a setting sun or reflections on wet pavement. A photograph from the next evening may help explain a concern if you clearly distinguish its conditions from those of the collision.

## Which records are worth requesting first?

Ask witnesses for contact details if they are willing. A bus passenger, delivery worker or customer might remember the event without knowing everyone’s names. If a business may have a recording, note its address, the direction of the camera and the time window to preserve. Include the minutes before impact. Footage that ends just before contact may still show how long the pedestrian was visible or when the vehicle began its turn.

Keep original photos and messages. A timestamped text saying “I’m outside the west entrance” can help place someone near a particular storefront. It does not prove that they were looking at a phone when they entered the road. Preserve the surrounding context and explain who sent or received it. Let the investigator decide what other information would be needed before drawing a conclusion about distraction.

You may need a family member to gather information while you receive treatment. Give them a narrow task, such as recording a witness’s contact information or locating the agency’s event number. Do not ask anyone to stand in traffic, reenact the crossing or confront the driver. ## How shared responsibility affects an injury claim

An insurer may point to an unsafe crossing when arguing that the injured person shares responsibility. Ask which conduct it relies on. Entering from behind a parked vehicle, ignoring a signal and walking in a visible lane for several seconds present different questions. Ask what evidence supports the insurer’s timeline and whether it considered the driver’s speed, lookout, turning path and opportunities to respond.

California’s comparative fault rules allow responsibility to be allocated between people whose conduct contributed to an injury. A pedestrian’s share can reduce the damages they recover. An allegation that the crossing was improper does not establish a particular percentage. Before responding to an allocation, write down the agreed facts and the disputed ones. A disagreement about when the driver could see the pedestrian requires different evidence from a disagreement about which signal was displayed. ## Keep the injury record separate from the crossing dispute

An insurer’s questions about where you crossed can consume most of a phone conversation. Continue documenting the effect of the injury in parallel. Keep appointment information, restrictions given by your treating clinicians, transport expenses and records of missed work. If bills arrive before the claim is resolved, see [how to track medical bills while an injury claim is pending](/blogs/medical-bills-while-car-accident-claim-pending-california). For a person who normally walks to a bus stop, a temporary inability to make that walk can affect the entire trip to work. Record what actually changed, when it changed and what alternative you used.

Suppose a relative drove you to three appointments and you paid for a rideshare on a fourth occasion. Keep the rideshare receipt and record your relative’s help separately. If your employer allowed you to work from home for part of a week, distinguish the hours you worked from the shifts you missed. The purpose of that record is to explain the injury’s practical effects without treating every disruption as the same kind of financial loss. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can an injured pedestrian have a claim after crossing outside a crosswalk?

Yes, depending on the circumstances. The crossing location matters, along with the proximity of traffic, the driver’s actions and the time available to avoid the impact. Record where you entered the street and how far you traveled before contact. If the insurer has already assigned responsibility, ask for its reasons in writing and keep the material you used to identify the crossing point.

### Does an unpainted intersection mean I was jaywalking?

No. Some intersections have unmarked crosswalks. Record the layout of the sidewalks, curb lines, traffic controls and any signs restricting crossing. If the driver or a witness used the word “jaywalking,” preserve the statement along with their description of what they saw. A description of the route is more informative than a conclusion about whether it was permitted.

### What if I cannot remember stepping off the curb?

Tell the person asking which part you remember and where the gap begins. A companion, independent witness or recording may help establish the missing sequence. Keep accounts from other people separate from your own memory. When you later review footage, note that the new detail came from the recording. You can still describe your injuries and care even when the crossing itself is uncertain.

### What should my family bring to a first conversation?

Bring the event number if available, any notice from an insurer, witness contacts, and a brief description of the crossing and current needs. A map with the start point and direction marked can help even if the distances are approximate. Identify which materials you already have and which are still pending.
