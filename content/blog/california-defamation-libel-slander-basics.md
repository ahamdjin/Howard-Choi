## Libel vs. slander: What counts as defamation in California?

California Civil Code sections 44 through 46 distinguish libel from slander. A printed article, social media post, or email can raise libel questions. A spoken accusation can raise slander questions. In either case, start with the exact words, who would understand them to refer to you, and what the listener or reader could reasonably take them to mean. A statement can identify you through specific context even when it omits your name. [Judicial Council of California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1700–1707](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

| Question | What you can document |
| --- | --- |
| What was said or shown? | Full text, an original message, the complete image and caption, or a listener’s account of the exact words |
| Who is it about? | Your name or identifying details, plus evidence explaining what recipients knew at the time |
| Who received it? | Recipient list, public post details, message delivery records, or a witness who actually read or heard it |
| What part is false? | Records showing whether the main factual accusation is true, such as dated messages, financial records, or a document that was misquoted |
| What happened afterward? | A customer’s written cancellation, a change in work assignments, or testimony about how the statement affected your reputation |

Publication has a narrower legal meaning than posting to a public website. A communication to another person who understood the statement and its reference may count. For example, a false accusation e-mailed to one customer goes to someone besides the subject of the statement. The same words in a message sent only to the person accused generally do not satisfy ordinary third-party publication. Record the actual recipient rather than estimating how far it might have spread. [California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1700–1705 and 1708](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

## Is the statement a factual accusation or a protected opinion?

Read the whole exchange. A reader may understand “I hated the service” as a personal judgment. A statement that an identifiable repair shop billed for an installed part and never installed it is a concrete allegation that records may help verify. Adding “in my opinion” leaves the question open if a reasonable reader would still understand the words as implying a verifiable fact. Context matters, including the forum, what came before and after, and whether the exchange uses exaggeration or figurative language. [Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep497/usrep497001/usrep497001.pdf).

Consider a neighborhood group discussing a volunteer. A member says the volunteer took the event’s cash and kept it. The assertion points to a specific event and can be investigated. A screenshot of only that sentence may miss a preceding message showing that the writer was repeating a fictional scenario or quoting an accusation to refute it. Save the thread in order and look for records of the cash handoff. You can then explain both why people would take the accusation seriously and why the substance was false.

### Whose fault matters, and under what standard?

The applicable standard can depend on whether the person bringing the claim is a public official, a public figure, or a private person, and whether the statement addresses a matter of public concern. For a covered statement about a public official or public figure, actual malice generally means the speaker knew it was false or recklessly disregarded whether it was false. Personal anger, an insult, or a history of disagreement does not by itself establish that standard. California’s jury instructions distinguish public-figure cases from private-figure cases and address when different standards apply to damages. [California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1700–1705](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

## Truth, privilege, and proof of harm

Substantial truth is a defense. A disagreement about a small detail may make little difference if the main imputation or gist is true. Privileges can also apply. California law protects certain statements made in judicial or other official proceedings and recognizes a qualified common-interest privilege in some communications. The audience, purpose, content, and circumstances matter. For example, a statement in a court filing and a later statement to a separate audience need to be evaluated in their own contexts. [California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1720, 1723, and 1724](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

Some categories of defamation are described as *per se*. For libel, the question can involve whether the statement is defamatory on its face, without needing additional facts to explain why. For slander, California law identifies particular categories, including some statements imputing crime or tending directly to injure a person in their occupation. The label can affect what kind of damages you have to show, and constitutional limits can still apply. Document actual impacts wherever you can. A customer saying why they canceled is generally more helpful than a revenue graph with no connection to the publication. [California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1700–1705](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

## What to save and when to act

1. **Preserve the original.** Save the entire page or message, account name, profile link, post URL, and visible dates. Keep copies of edits or deleted versions you already lawfully possess. For speech, ask witnesses to save a dated account in their own words.
2. **Separate the dates.** Write down when the statement appeared, when you learned about it, when there was an edit or new share, and when you experienced any impact. Keep the time zone if you have it.
3. **Gather records about accuracy and audience.** Save contemporaneous records about the event and evidence of who received or understood the statement. Avoid sending the accusation on to more people solely to make a record.
4. **Identify what you want to achieve.** Removal, a correction reaching the same audience, and monetary recovery are different outcomes. Save the evidence before requesting a platform takedown or correction. If a news publication or broadcast is involved, ask a lawyer promptly about California Civil Code section 48a. It can affect damages and has its own timing requirements. [California civil jury instructions, CACI No. 1709](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

California Code of Civil Procedure section 340(c) generally allows one year to sue for libel or slander. Online publication raises timing questions when you discover an older post, see it in search results, or encounter an edited or re-shared version. California’s single-publication rule, Civil Code section 3425.3, can affect whether one publication gives rise to one claim. Preserve the original and later versions and have the deadline evaluated using the actual facts. A platform complaint or private request for removal should never be treated as a court filing. [California Courts filing deadlines](https://www.selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/statute-limitations) · [California civil jury instructions, CACI No. 1722](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

Before suing, ask how California’s anti-SLAPP law, Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, could apply to the statements at issue. If an action arises from qualifying speech or petitioning activity, an early motion can test whether the claim has enough legal and evidentiary support to proceed. The statute also has attorney fee consequences. A person deciding whether to sue should review the original words and available evidence with a lawyer who handles defamation. [California Supreme Court on section 425.16](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/S239686.PDF).

If a physical injury is part of the same dispute, our [California personal injury guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-guide) explains the separate steps for an injury claim. Ask a lawyer which deadlines apply to each claim.

## How to make a record a lawyer can review

Make a short timeline, then attach the original documents. In the first row, write the earliest date you can substantiate and link the exact post or message. Add separate rows for later versions, responses, and losses. Put the source next to each date. Mark approximate dates as estimates. For example, if you learned about a post when a client forwarded it, keep both the original post date if visible and the dated forwarding message.

For each witness, record how they encountered the statement and what led them to believe it referred to you. A coworker who was in the group chat may be able to describe the audience and explain a nickname. A customer who heard about it later may only know what someone else told them. Both accounts can be relevant, and the distinction helps establish which portions each person can describe firsthand.

If the statement repeats several accusations, list them separately. A text accusing a contractor of taking a deposit and missing an agreed deadline contains two claims that can be checked against different records. Include facts and documents that cut both ways. A lawyer can then assess which statements could support a claim, which evidence is still missing, and whether a correction, a targeted platform report, or litigation fits the situation.

If you lost work or customers, keep the facts that connect each loss to the statement. A canceled contract with a written explanation can be stronger evidence of that connection than a broad dip in revenue. Preserve invoices, earlier bookings, scheduling changes, and any written reasons from the person who made the decision. Record competing explanations if you know them, such as an unrelated budget cut. The same approach helps if your reputation suffered without an obvious financial loss. Write down who changed their dealings with you, when, and what they said about the accusation.

## Frequently asked questions about libel and slander

### Can a false statement in a private group chat be defamation?

Potentially. Publication can occur when people other than the subject read the statement and understand whom it describes. A group chat’s privacy setting is one fact about its audience. Save the full conversation and evidence of who belonged to the chat when the statement was sent. [California civil jury instructions, CACI Nos. 1700–1705](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

### Is a bad review defamation if it costs me customers?

It depends on what the review communicates. A personal judgment about service is different from a specific false factual accusation. Preserve the full review and the records relevant to the factual claim. If a customer canceled because of the review, keep their message explaining why. [Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep497/usrep497001/usrep497001.pdf).

### What if the account that posted about me is anonymous?

Save the post and profile URLs, visible handle, full conversation, and date before the account changes or disappears. Ask a defamation lawyer what lawful identification options are available and what evidence a court might require. Similar usernames, writing styles, or timing can give you reasons to ask questions, but are insufficient on their own to reliably identify a person.

### Does deleting or correcting a post end a defamation claim?

A correction may reduce ongoing harm and can matter to the remedies available. It does not erase the history of who received the original statement. Keep both versions and record whether the correction reached the same audience. Ask about special California rules if a news publication or broadcast is involved. [California civil jury instructions, CACI No. 1709](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

### Does a recent share restart the California filing deadline?

A new share does not automatically restart the deadline for the original publication. Whether a later act amounts to a legally separate publication depends on what was communicated, who sent it, and the surrounding facts. Save distinct copies and dates and seek a deadline assessment promptly. [California Courts filing deadlines](https://www.selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/statute-limitations) · [California civil jury instructions, CACI No. 1722](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).

### Will asking for a correction make the filing deadline pause?

Do not count on it. Keep track of the date separately, even if the writer, employer, or platform says it will investigate. A correction request can serve a different purpose, and certain claims involving news publications or broadcasts have separate rules. [California Courts filing deadlines](https://www.selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/civil-lawsuit/statute-limitations) · [California civil jury instructions, CACI No. 1709](https://courts.ca.gov/system/files/file/judicial_council_of_california_civil_jury_instructions_2026.pdf).
