## Start with safety and appropriate medical care

If anyone may be injured, safety and appropriate medical attention come before building a claim. Some symptoms are obvious immediately; others become clearer after the initial shock of the collision has passed. A medical record created close in time to the crash can also help explain when symptoms began and how treatment developed.

Keep the medical story accurate. Describe what you are actually experiencing, follow the treatment plan you receive, and keep appointment information, referrals, imaging, prescriptions, and work restrictions together. The goal is not to make an injury sound worse than it is. The goal is to create a reliable record of what happened and how recovery progressed.

## Preserve the scene before it changes

When it can be done safely, photograph the vehicles, visible damage, roadway, lane markings, traffic controls, debris, lighting, skid marks, nearby businesses, and the wider scene. A location that looks obvious on the day of the crash may look very different weeks later.

Keep names and contact information for drivers and witnesses, insurance details, police or collision-report information, towing and storage records, and anything showing the exact time and location. If a nearby business, residence, intersection, rideshare vehicle, or dash camera may have recorded the collision, identifying that source early can matter because video is not always kept indefinitely.

## Keep insurance communications organized

Different insurance companies may contact you for different reasons. Keep a simple log of the company, representative, date, phone number or email, what was requested, and what you sent. Save claim numbers, letters, emails, text messages, repair estimates, rental-car information, and benefit explanations.

Do not assume the first policy you hear about is the only coverage that matters. Depending on the facts, coverage questions can involve the at-fault driver, the vehicle owner, an employer, a commercial policy, a rideshare policy, or your own uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage.

## Document work loss and the practical effect of the injury

Medical bills are only one part of an injury claim. Keep records of missed work, reduced hours, modified duties, lost commissions or opportunities, transportation costs, help needed at home, follow-up appointments, and other accident-related expenses.

Daily limitations can also be relevant. Specific examples are more useful than broad statements: difficulty driving, lifting, sleeping, caring for children, exercising, walking, sitting for long periods, or performing ordinary household tasks can help explain what changed during recovery.

## Do not ignore fault questions

California follows comparative fault, which means responsibility can be divided between people involved in an accident. If the other driver or insurer disputes fault, scene photographs, witness accounts, vehicle damage, video, roadway evidence, and consistent statements about how the collision happened can become especially important.

Do not guess about details you do not remember. A clear record of what you know, what you observed, and what still needs to be confirmed is more useful than trying to fill every gap immediately.

## Know that California deadlines exist

California Courts explains that a personal injury lawsuit generally has a two-year statute of limitations from the date of injury, but exceptions can change the calculation. Claims involving a state or local government can have much shorter notice requirements. Property-damage deadlines can also differ from personal-injury deadlines.

That is why the deadline should be identified from the actual facts rather than assumed from a general rule. Waiting until the end of the limitation period can also make evidence harder to obtain even when the lawsuit deadline has not yet expired.

## Know when a legal review becomes useful

A legal review can be especially useful when injuries are significant, fault is disputed, there may be more than one responsible party, an insurer questions treatment or causation, future care is expected, work has been affected, policy limits may be an issue, or important evidence needs to be preserved.

A useful first consultation does not require a perfect file. The accident date and location, treatment status, photographs, insurance information, police or incident-report details, and communications you already have are usually enough to begin identifying the next questions.
