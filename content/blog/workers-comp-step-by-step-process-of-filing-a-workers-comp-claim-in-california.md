Start with the reporting and filing steps below. Treatment, disability payments, medical evaluations, and return-to-work discussions can then overlap. They do not always happen in a fixed sequence.

## Keep a copy of each handoff

Keep the date you reported the injury, the date you received the DWC 1, and proof of when you returned it. Add any work restrictions, treatment authorizations, and claim notices as they arrive. If a document is late or missing, that record gives the claims administrator or a DWC Information and Assistance officer a specific point to investigate. [California DWC claim guide](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dwc/FileAClaim.htm)

## Understand what workers’ compensation covers

California workers’ compensation generally provides benefits for employees with work-related injuries or illnesses without requiring proof of employer negligence. A claim can involve a single accident, repeated physical activity, or workplace exposure. Depending on eligibility, benefits can include medical treatment, temporary disability, permanent disability, supplemental job displacement benefits, and death benefits for eligible dependents. [California DWC worker FAQs](https://dir.ca.gov/dwc/WCFaqIW.html)

Workers’ compensation generally does not pay damages for pain and suffering. Some workplace accidents also support a separate claim against someone other than the employer, such as an equipment manufacturer. That possibility requires its own liability and deadline review. Do not assume the workers’ compensation claim preserves a separate lawsuit. The [California personal injury deadline guide](/blogs/california-personal-injury-deadlines) concerns separate civil claims; it does not replace the DWC and WCAB deadlines discussed below.

A workplace accident may also involve a driver, property controller, or equipment manufacturer outside the employment relationship. The [blunt-trauma liability guide](/blogs/blunt-force-trauma-determining-liability-in-blunt-trauma-lawsuits) explains how causation and fault evidence differ from medical documentation. Our [serious injury practice page](/practice-areas/serious-injuries) describes civil injury representation; confirm directly whether any proposed engagement includes a separate workers’ compensation proceeding. [DWC injured worker guidebook](https://www.dir.ca.gov/InjuredWorkerGuidebook/InjuredWorkerGuidebook.pdf)

## Report the injury and start the claim

### Notify your employer promptly

For a work-related road collision, the [California after-crash checklist](/blogs/what-to-do-after-a-car-accident-in-california) covers scene and vehicle records. A fatal workplace incident may involve different benefits and potential civil claims; the [wrongful-death consultation guide](/blogs/buena-park-wrongful-death-attorney) explains the family records to bring without assuming every workplace death supports a lawsuit.

Tell your supervisor or another appropriate employer representative when and where the injury occurred, what happened, and which body parts are affected. For symptoms that developed over time, describe the work activities or exposure and when you first noticed the problem.

California generally requires reporting within 30 days. Delayed notice can jeopardize benefits, although the employer’s knowledge and other exceptions can affect the result. Report symptoms promptly rather than waiting for a diagnosis or assuming a coworker has notified management.

### Get appropriate medical care

Call 911 or seek emergency care when necessary. Do not wait for a claim form before obtaining urgent treatment. Tell the provider that the condition may be work-related and identify your employer.

For nonemergency care, ask the employer or claims administrator where to obtain treatment. Your employer may use a medical provider network, or MPN. A properly predesignated personal physician can treat you in qualifying circumstances, but having a regular doctor does not by itself establish that right.

Describe the tasks you cannot perform safely, not just your pain level. For example, explain whether gripping a tool, standing through a shift, or lifting a particular load worsens symptoms. Ask for written work restrictions so the employer can evaluate actual duties.

### Complete and return the DWC-1

For a qualifying work injury, including one requiring more than first aid or causing lost time beyond the injury date, the employer must provide a DWC-1 claim form within one working day after learning of it. If the form is not provided, obtain it from the Division of Workers’ Compensation, or DWC. Complete and sign the employee section, describe the injury accurately, and identify all affected body parts you know about. Return it promptly and retain proof of delivery.

The DWC-1 starts the claim process with the employer. An **Application for Adjudication of Claim** opens a case with the WCAB. These are different documents serving different purposes. A one-year limitation can apply to initiating WCAB proceedings, but the calculation can depend on the injury, benefits paid, treatment furnished, and other circumstances. Do not wait for negotiations to end before checking that deadline.

### Confirm the employer’s next steps

After receiving your completed employee section, the employer must complete its section, provide you with a copy, and forward the form to the claims administrator within one working day. Obtain the administrator’s contact information and your claim number. The administrator may work for an insurer or handle claims for a self-insured employer.

Create one claim file containing the following records.

- Your injury report, DWC-1, and delivery confirmation.
- Medical reports, treatment instructions, and work restrictions.
- Pay records showing usual earnings and income lost after the injury.
- Claim notices and envelopes showing mailing dates.
- Receipts and mileage records for potentially reimbursable expenses.
- A dated contact log recording requests, responses, and promised actions.

This file helps you distinguish an unanswered request from a formal decision and gives an adviser the documents needed to assess deadlines.

## Match the problem to the correct procedure

Before responding to a notice, identify what it actually decides. A claim denial, a treatment denial, and a disagreement about disability can require different responses. Use this table to choose the next question to investigate, rather than treating every problem as an insurance appeal.

| Situation or document | What it means | Practical next step |
|---|---|---|
| You reported the injury but have not submitted a DWC-1 | The employer has notice, but you have not completed the claim-form step | Obtain and return the form. Separately check whether a WCAB filing is needed.  |
| A delay notice says the claim is under investigation | The administrator has not yet accepted or denied responsibility | Identify the stated decision date, missing information, and arrangements for authorized treatment. Ask whether special investigation rules apply.  |
| A letter denies that the injury is work-related | The dispute concerns responsibility for the claim | Review the reasons and medical evidence with an attorney or Information and Assistance officer. Check the WCAB filing deadline.  |
| Utilization review denies or modifies requested treatment | The dispute concerns the medical necessity of particular care | Read the notice immediately for independent medical review instructions and the submission deadline. A complaint to the adjuster is not a substitute.  |
| You disagree with a medical opinion about causation, restrictions, or lasting disability | A medical-legal evaluation process may be needed | Ask which QME or AME procedure applies before selecting an evaluator or missing an objection deadline.  |
| A judge has issued a decision you dispute | A formal review deadline may already be running | Obtain advice about reconsideration or another appropriate review procedure immediately. Do not rely on an informal request to reopen discussions.  |

## Follow the investigation and treatment decisions

The administrator may review medical records, employment information, witness accounts, and your description of the injury. Read each notice for the issue being investigated and any information requested. If a request is unclear, ask what document or answer is needed.

Within one working day after you file the DWC-1, the employer must authorize appropriate treatment under the workers’ compensation rules. During the investigation, treatment costs can be covered up to $10,000 before the claim is accepted or denied. This protection is not a cash allowance or unrestricted permission to arrange any treatment independently. Ask how authorized appointments and referrals will be scheduled.

Under the general rule, an injury is presumed covered if the claim is not denied within 90 days after the claim form is filed. Exceptions and special rules can affect that period. If you receive a delay notice, ask the administrator or an adviser to confirm the deadline for your claim, particularly if an occupation-specific presumption may apply. A presumption of coverage does not establish entitlement to every requested benefit.

## Check wage replacement and lasting disability separately

### Temporary disability benefits

Temporary disability replaces part of qualifying lost wages during recovery. Temporary total disability generally applies when the injury prevents work. Temporary partial disability can apply when you work but earn less because of the injury.

Payments generally equal two-thirds of qualifying gross wages, subject to statutory minimums, maximums, waiting periods, and duration limits. The injury date matters. Compare the administrator’s calculation with your pay records, especially if your earnings varied or you held more than one job.

A worker returning to shorter shifts, for example, should report both the hours worked and actual earnings. Reduced hours alone do not establish the benefit amount. Medical restrictions, wage loss, and eligibility rules must be considered together.

### Medical evaluations and permanent disability

Your treating doctor assesses treatment needs, work restrictions, and recovery. When the condition becomes permanent and stationary, also called maximum medical improvement, the doctor evaluates lasting impairment and future care needs. Reaching that stage does not necessarily mean you are symptom-free or no longer need treatment.

A qualified medical evaluator, or QME, can assess disputed medical issues through the required selection process. If you have an attorney, the parties may agree on an agreed medical evaluator, or AME. Represented workers can also use the QME process when appropriate. Neither process should be understood as simply accepting whichever doctor the insurer selects. Selection and objection deadlines require attention.

Medical impairment and permanent disability are not interchangeable. Impairment findings contribute to a disability rating, which can also reflect age, occupation, and legal adjustments. Permanent disability payments do not necessarily replace all future lost earnings. Review the underlying medical report, not just the proposed payment amount.

## Compare proposed work with your restrictions

Give the employer current written restrictions and request a description of proposed regular, modified, or alternative work. Compare specific tasks, hours, lifting demands, and repetitive movements with the doctor’s instructions. A “light-duty” label does not establish that a job is medically appropriate.

If an assignment conflicts with restrictions, document the task and promptly seek clarification from the employer and treating doctor. For example, a shoulder restriction may be inconsistent with an assignment that still requires repeated overhead lifting.

Workers’ compensation does not guarantee a particular job, and unavailable modified work does not automatically establish continuing temporary disability eligibility. Separate accommodation laws may also matter. Eligible workers can qualify for a supplemental job displacement voucher when the statutory conditions are met.

## Evaluate settlement by responsibility, not just payment size

Two common settlement structures handle future obligations differently. **Stipulations with Request for Award** generally establish matters such as permanent disability while preserving covered future medical care under the award. A **Compromise and Release** provides a negotiated lump sum resolving specified liabilities, often including future medical care. A workers’ compensation judge must approve the agreement.

Before comparing offers, work through the following questions using the proposed agreement and medical reports.

| Question | Why it changes the comparison |
|---|---|
| What treatment does the doctor anticipate? | Continuing prescriptions, specialist visits, or possible procedures affect the significance of keeping medical coverage open. |
| Who will authorize and pay for that care? | An award preserving covered care and a settlement closing medical liability leave you with different responsibilities. |
| Which issues remain disputed? | A payment figure alone does not show whether the agreement resolves disability, unpaid benefits, future care, or all specified claims. |
| What amount will you actually receive? | Review fees, advances, and other stated deductions rather than relying on the headline settlement figure. |
| How might the agreement affect other benefits? | Ask for an individualized review of benefit interactions before committing to terms. |

Use these questions to identify missing information, not to calculate a universal “fair” settlement. Uncertain future care deserves particular attention because a lump sum and continuing access to covered treatment serve different needs.

## Get help with disputes before deadlines expire

For a denied claim, unpaid benefits, or disputed disability, WCAB proceedings may be necessary. Treatment disputes can instead involve utilization review and independent medical review. A disagreement about a medical opinion can require the QME or AME process. Pursuing the wrong route can waste time without preserving the correct deadline.

DWC Information and Assistance officers help workers understand forms, procedures, and available resources. An attorney can assess disputed evidence, filing deadlines, benefit calculations, and settlement terms. When seeking help, provide the notice you received, its envelope, your DWC-1, and relevant medical reports. Confirm that a lawyer accepts workers’ compensation matters before seeking representation; a personal injury practice does not establish that it handles WCAB proceedings.

## Frequently asked questions

### What if my employer refuses to provide or process the DWC-1?

Obtain the form from the DWC and return your completed employee section using a method that documents delivery. If mailing it, the DWC recommends certified mail with a return receipt. Contact an Information and Assistance officer if the employer will not cooperate. Ask about both claim processing and any separate WCAB filing needed to protect your rights.

### What if I reported the injury late because symptoms developed gradually?

Report the condition as soon as you recognize a possible connection to work. Record when symptoms began, what work activities you believe contributed, and when a medical professional discussed that connection. Gradual injuries can raise different injury-date and deadline questions from a single accident. Do not assume the first symptom date alone determines every deadline, or that a late report automatically defeats the claim.

### Can the insurer deny treatment after accepting my claim?

Yes. Acceptance of the injury does not automatically approve every proposed treatment. A doctor’s request can undergo utilization review for medical necessity. If treatment is denied or modified, read the decision for independent medical review instructions and deadlines. Confirm whether the disagreement concerns medical necessity or another issue, because the proper procedure depends on that distinction.

### Can I change doctors if I am unhappy with my treatment?

Your options depend on whether care is provided through an MPN and which physician-selection rules apply. Within an MPN, you generally have options to change treating doctors, and separate procedures address disagreements about diagnosis or treatment. Ask for the network’s written instructions before arranging outside care. An unauthorized change can create a payment dispute.

### What if a return-to-work offer exceeds my medical restrictions?

Give the employer the current work-status report and identify the specific conflicting duties. Ask the treating doctor to review the proposed tasks and clarify restrictions where needed. Do not assume the job title resolves the issue. Whether an offer affects benefits depends on the medical evidence, the work offered, and the benefit rules, so obtain advice before making a decision based solely on benefit concerns.

### Can I receive permanent disability benefits while working?

Yes, returning to work does not by itself eliminate eligibility for permanent disability benefits. Those benefits address qualifying lasting disability rather than simply whether you remain employed. Payment timing and the amount due can depend on the rating and other statutory rules. Ask the administrator to explain any notice withholding, advancing, or changing payments.

### Does keeping future medical care open mean every future treatment will be paid?

No. An award preserving future medical care generally covers treatment for the work injury under workers’ compensation rules. Requested care can still be reviewed for medical necessity, and disputes can arise over whether a condition is related to the injury. Before agreeing to a settlement, distinguish continuing medical coverage from an unconditional promise to pay every future medical expense.
