## Start with economic losses

Economic damages can include medical expenses, lost wages, future medical care, future income or earning loss, and other reasonably supported financial consequences of the injury. The important distinction is between an amount that is merely possible and an amount that can be tied to the accident with records or reliable evidence.

Current bills are usually easier to document than future losses. Future surgery, rehabilitation, medication, reduced earning capacity, or long-term care often requires medical or vocational support rather than a rough guess.

## Non-economic harm is not one fixed multiplier

Pain, physical limitations, inconvenience, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, and other non-economic effects can matter in an injury claim, but California law does not require a universal medical-bill multiplier for every case.

The nature of the injury, duration of symptoms, invasiveness of treatment, permanent limitations, scarring, recovery time, credibility of the record, and effect on ordinary activities can all change how non-economic harm is evaluated.

## Fault can reduce the practical recovery

California comparative fault can reduce damages by the percentage of responsibility assigned to the injured person. That means a strong damages case can still have a lower practical value when liability is heavily disputed.

The reverse is also true: clear liability does not automatically create a high-value case when the injury is minor, resolves quickly, or the claimed losses are poorly supported. Liability and damages are separate parts of the analysis.

## Insurance and collectability matter

A case can have substantial theoretical damages but limited practical recovery if there is not enough applicable insurance or other collectible coverage. Depending on the accident, there may be more than one policy or responsible party, including vehicle owners, employers, businesses, commercial carriers, rideshare coverage, umbrella policies, or uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage.

Identifying those sources early is part of evaluating the claim rather than something that should wait until the end.

## Liens and reimbursement can affect the net result

The gross settlement or verdict is not always the amount the injured person ultimately receives. Medical liens, health-plan reimbursement rights, litigation costs, attorney fees, outstanding medical balances, or other deductions may affect the net recovery.

That is one reason two cases with the same headline settlement can create very different outcomes for the people involved.

## Evidence changes how persuasive the damages are

Medical records, imaging, photographs, witness accounts, wage records, expert opinions, treatment recommendations, incident reports, video, and a consistent timeline can make the claim easier to evaluate. Missing or contradictory evidence can create questions even when an injury is genuine.

A useful case-value analysis therefore asks not only what happened, but what can be demonstrated and how clearly the evidence connects the accident to the claimed loss.

## Use a calculator as a starting point, not a promise

A personal injury calculator can help organize medical expenses, wages, treatment severity, future losses, and comparative fault into a rough educational range. It cannot see insurance limits, liens, disputed causation, venue, witness credibility, evidence quality, negotiation posture, or the many factual details that can move a real case.

The best use of an estimator is to understand which inputs matter and identify the questions that need a closer review—not to treat the output as a settlement offer or guaranteed result.
