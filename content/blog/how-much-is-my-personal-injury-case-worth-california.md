This is the first question almost everybody asks, and the honest answer is that nobody can tell you on a first phone call. Anyone who gives you a number before seeing your medical records is guessing, and usually guessing high to get the case.

What can be explained is what the number is built from, and why two people with the same diagnosis can end up in very different places.

## Start with what you can actually count

Economic losses are the part with paperwork behind them. Medical bills already incurred, treatment you still need, lost wages, and reduced earning capacity if the injury changes what work you can do.

The one people undercount is future care. If your doctor expects you to need injections every eighteen months, or a knee replacement in a decade, that is part of the claim now. It does not get added later. Settling before anyone has assessed what the next ten years look like is the most expensive mistake in this whole area, and it is usually made by people who are keen to be done with it.

## The other half has no invoice

Pain, limitation, and the parts of your life the injury took away are real losses, and there is no formula that converts them into dollars.

You may have read about a multiplier, where non-economic damages come out as some multiple of the medical bills. Adjusters do sometimes work that way internally as a starting point. It is not a rule, it is not law, and it breaks down completely in cases where the treatment was cheap but the consequences were not. A fracture that heals badly can cost very little to treat and change how you walk for good.

What moves this part is specificity. "It still hurts" does very little. "I have not slept through the night since March, I cannot lift my daughter, and I gave up the softball league I had played in for nine years" does a great deal more, because it is checkable and it is human.

## Fault reduces the number, it does not erase it

California divides responsibility rather than assigning it entirely to one side. If you are found twenty percent responsible, you recover eighty percent.

This matters more than people expect because the other side's insurer has every reason to argue your share up. Evidence about how the crash actually happened is therefore not just about winning or losing, it is about the size of the eventual check.

## Insurance limits can cap everything

This is the part nobody wants to hear. A claim can be worth a great deal on paper and still be limited by how much coverage exists.

California's minimum liability coverage is low. If the at-fault driver carries the minimum and your medical bills exceed it, the value of your claim may be constrained by their policy rather than by your injury. That is when it becomes important to look for other sources: their employer if they were working, the vehicle's owner, an umbrella policy, or your own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, which a lot of people carry without realizing it.

## Liens come off the top

If your health insurer, Medicare, Medi-Cal, or a medical provider on a lien paid for your treatment, they may have a right to be reimbursed out of your settlement.

This is why the headline number and the amount you actually take home can be quite different, and why a firm quoting you a gross figure without discussing liens is not giving you the full picture. Negotiating those liens down is real work and it directly affects your net.

## Evidence is what makes any of it persuasive

Two claims with identical facts settle differently depending on how well the file is built. Consistent treatment records, a doctor who documents limitations rather than just visits, wage records rather than estimates, and photographs that show what happened all make a number defensible instead of arguable.

The reverse is also true. Gaps in treatment, a vague medical chart, and a client who cannot say concretely what changed will get discounted, whatever the injury was.

## Use a calculator as a conversation starter

An online estimate, including ours, works from the few facts you type into it. It cannot see your MRI, does not know what your doctor expects in five years, and has no idea what the other driver's policy limits are.

It is useful for getting a rough sense of scale before a first conversation. It is not a valuation, and no honest lawyer will treat it as one.


## Separate a claim's four distinct questions

A useful case assessment asks: what losses can be supported; who caused them under applicable law; which insurance or other resources may be available; and what costs or reimbursement obligations might affect a person's net recovery. Combining these into a single settlement multiplier can obscure the actual decision. A serious injury may produce substantial supported damages but still involve disputed responsibility or inadequate coverage.

Create separate tabs or lists for already-incurred medical bills, future medical recommendations, wages already missed, and any supported future work impact. Then maintain a different list for the insurers and relevant liability facts. A worksheet that separates categories prevents a preliminary estimate from being mistaken for a promised outcome.

## What a loss-of-income record should look like

For hourly employees, preserve schedules, pay stubs, wage rate, employer confirmation of missed shifts and written work restrictions. For salaried workers, record paid leave consumed, reduced duties and any loss of incentive pay. For self-employed people, contracts, invoices, calendar changes and past earnings trends may help distinguish accident-related losses from ordinary business fluctuations.

Future earning capacity is more complicated than multiplying today's missed wages by a number of years. Age, employment history, realistic career path, medically supported limitations and potentially vocational expert evidence may matter. The claim should distinguish a documented restriction from a hypothetical permanent disability.

## Why a future-care estimate requires evidence

Future surgery, rehabilitation, medication, assistive devices or home modifications can be important where appropriately supported. Not every mentioned option is a medically recommended plan, however. Record who recommended it, when it may be necessary, the expected course and any uncertainty about the prognosis. Medical professionals must supply clinical judgment; legal counsel evaluates the evidentiary and legal requirements.

Pain and other non-economic losses are not the same as reimbursing a medical invoice. The factual record may include sleep, mobility, family responsibilities and activities the person could perform before and after the injury. A clear example of an actual limitation is more useful than a generic score assigned by an online quiz.

## An example of gross losses versus actual recovery

Suppose two injured people both have $15,000 in past medical charges. The first returns to work without restriction after a short course of care; the second has medically documented rehabilitation needs and several months of lost work. Their financial and functional histories differ even though the initial bills match. Now add a dispute about accident fault or a low liability policy limit and their practical outcomes can diverge further.

This example does not assign a case value or predict a settlement. It shows why the relevant records, defendant, insurer and timing are the inputs—and why any educational calculator must explain what it cannot know.
