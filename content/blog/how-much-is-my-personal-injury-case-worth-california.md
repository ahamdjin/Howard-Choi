A responsible estimate usually requires much more than a diagnosis and the collision date. Without records showing treatment, actual loss, disputed fault and available insurance, any early number is preliminary; it should be explained as an estimate, not a promised outcome.

What can be explained is what the number is built from, and why two people with the same diagnosis can end up in very different places.

## Start with what you can actually count

Economic losses are the part with paperwork behind them. Medical bills already incurred, treatment you still need, lost wages, and reduced earning capacity if the injury changes what work you can do.

The one people undercount is future care. If medical evidence supports a reasonably certain future need, such as rehabilitation, additional treatment or an assistive device, potential future costs may be relevant to the claim. The timing and necessity should be supported by appropriate medical opinions; neither an unconfirmed possibility nor an online estimate automatically establishes a recoverable expense.

## The other half has no invoice

Pain, limitation, and the parts of your life the injury took away are real losses, and there is no formula that converts them into dollars.

You may have read about a multiplier, where non-economic damages come out as some multiple of the medical bills. Adjusters do sometimes work that way internally as a starting point. It is not a rule, it is not law, and it breaks down completely in cases where the treatment was cheap but the consequences were not. A fracture that heals badly can cost very little to treat and change how you walk for good.

What moves this part is specificity. "It still hurts" does very little. "I have not slept through the night since March, I cannot lift my daughter, and I gave up the softball league I had played in for nine years" does a great deal more, because it is checkable and it is human.

## Fault reduces the number, it does not erase it

California divides responsibility rather than assigning it entirely to one side. If you are found twenty percent responsible, you recover eighty percent.

This matters more than people expect because the other side's insurer has every reason to argue your share up. Evidence about how the crash actually happened is therefore not just about winning or losing, it is about the size of the eventual check.

## Insurance limits can cap everything

A claim may have strongly supported damages while the available insurance or realistically collectible assets are limited. Coverage and legal damages are related, but they are not the same amount.

California's minimum liability coverage is low. If the at-fault driver carries the minimum and your medical bills exceed it, the value of your claim may be constrained by their policy rather than by your injury. That is when it becomes important to look for other sources: their employer if they were working, the vehicle's owner, an umbrella policy, or your own uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage, which a lot of people carry without realizing it.

## Liens come off the top

A health insurer, Medicare, Medi-Cal, or a medical provider may assert a reimbursement right or lien depending on the program, contract and circumstances. The amount and validity of a particular demand need to be checked rather than assumed.

This is why the headline number and the amount you actually take home can be quite different, and why a firm quoting you a gross figure without discussing liens is not giving you the full picture. Negotiating those liens down is real work and it directly affects your net.

## Evidence is what makes any of it persuasive

Two claims with identical facts settle differently depending on how well the file is built. Consistent treatment records, a doctor who documents limitations rather than just visits, wage records rather than estimates, and photographs that show what happened all make a number defensible instead of arguable.

The reverse is also true. Gaps in treatment, a vague medical chart, and a client who cannot say concretely what changed will get discounted, whatever the injury was.

## Use a calculator as a conversation starter

An online estimate, including ours, works from the few facts you type into it. It cannot see your MRI, does not know what your doctor expects in five years, and has no idea what the other driver's policy limits are.

It is useful for getting a rough sense of scale before a first conversation. It is not a valuation, and no honest lawyer will treat it as one.


## Separate a claim's four distinct questions

A useful case assessment asks: what losses can be supported; who caused them under applicable law; which insurance or other resources may be available; and what costs or reimbursement obligations might affect a person's net recovery. Combining these into a single settlement multiplier can obscure the actual decision. A serious injury may produce substantial supported damages but still involve disputed responsibility or inadequate coverage.

Create separate tabs or lists for already-incurred medical bills, future medical recommendations, wages already missed, and any supported future work impact. Then maintain a different list for the insurers and relevant liability facts. A worksheet that separates categories prevents a preliminary estimate from being mistaken for a promised outcome.

## What a loss-of-income record should look like

For hourly employees, preserve schedules, pay stubs, wage rate, employer confirmation of missed shifts and written work restrictions. For salaried workers, record paid leave consumed, reduced duties and any loss of incentive pay. For self-employed people, contracts, invoices, calendar changes and past earnings trends may help distinguish accident-related losses from ordinary business fluctuations.

Future earning capacity is more complicated than multiplying today's missed wages by a number of years. Age, employment history, realistic career path, medically supported limitations and potentially vocational expert evidence may matter. The claim should distinguish a documented restriction from a hypothetical permanent disability.

## Why a future-care estimate requires evidence

Future surgery, rehabilitation, medication, assistive devices or home modifications can be important where appropriately supported. Not every mentioned option is a medically recommended plan, however. Record who recommended it, when it may be necessary, the expected course and any uncertainty about the prognosis. Medical professionals must supply clinical judgment; legal counsel evaluates the evidentiary and legal requirements.

Pain and other non-economic losses are not the same as reimbursing a medical invoice. The factual record may include sleep, mobility, family responsibilities and activities the person could perform before and after the injury. A clear example of an actual limitation is more useful than a generic score assigned by an online quiz.

## An example of gross losses versus actual recovery

Suppose two injured people both have $15,000 in past medical charges. The first returns to work without restriction after a short course of care; the second has medically documented rehabilitation needs and several months of lost work. Their financial and functional histories differ even though the initial bills match. Now add a dispute about accident fault or a low liability policy limit and their practical outcomes can diverge further.

This example does not assign a case value or predict a settlement. It shows why the relevant records, defendant, insurer and timing are the inputs—and why any educational calculator must explain what it cannot know.

## California jury instructions: what a damages figure must be built from

California's official civil jury instructions separate several categories of loss. CACI No. 3903A addresses past and future medical expenses and explains that future care must be reasonably certain to be needed, with reasonable costs supported. CACI No. 3903C concerns past and future lost earnings. CACI No. 3903D separately addresses loss of earning capacity, such as a medically supported reduction in the ability to earn money over time.

These categories are different from estimating 'pain and suffering' by multiplying invoices. The non-economic impact can involve physical pain, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment or changes in ordinary life, depending on the claim and what the law allows. A useful account ties those effects to specific activities and testimony, not to a universally valid multiplier.

The documents needed also differ. A provider's detailed recommendation supports a future-care question; pay records and work restrictions support missed earnings; a vocational or economic assessment may be necessary for a significant long-term work-capacity claim. Avoid double counting the same loss under multiple headings.

## How California's 2025 insurance minimums affect the conversation

The California Department of Insurance states that standard automobile liability minimums rose in 2025 to $30,000 for bodily injury to one person, $60,000 for all bodily injury per accident, and $15,000 for property damage. These are mandatory minimum policy limits in the standard category, not a formula for evaluating injury damages. Some drivers have higher limits, commercial or other coverage; special programs have different terms. Even when an insurer confirms a bodily-injury limit, that does not prove the liability insurer owes the full limit.

Ask what the declarations page actually shows, whether other responsible parties or policies may apply, and whether uninsured/underinsured motorist or medical-payments coverage is available under your own policy. Only a fact-specific review can address exclusions, covered drivers, exhaustion, consent requirements and payment priority. A $30,000 limit is a potential payment ceiling for that coverage, not evidence that a particular injury is worth $30,000.

## A two-column damages worksheet that is actually useful

Create one column for losses already documented and another for possible future losses requiring more evidence. For past medical care, record provider, date, type of service, charges and payments, with originals retained. For wages, document dates missed, regular rate, time off, employer verification and medical restrictions. For out-of-pocket costs, attach receipts and explain how each expense relates to the accident.

In the future-loss column, write the proposed item and the evidence still needed: a surgeon's recommendation, treatment plan, functional assessment or employer/vocational information. Label a recommendation as a recommendation, and a possibility as a possibility. This method prevents an insurer's demand spreadsheet from quietly converting an uncertain estimate into an asserted medical fact.

Keep non-economic effects in a separate dated log. Specific examples—difficulty walking a normal commute, the number of classes missed, needing help carrying groceries—are more informative than general adjectives. The log is not a clinical record and should not substitute for discussing symptoms with healthcare professionals.

## Gross settlement versus net distribution: a worked example

Suppose a hypothetical negotiated gross settlement is $100,000. That is not the injured person's guaranteed take-home sum. Depending on the fee contract and actual case, attorney fees, reimbursable litigation expenses and valid negotiated healthcare reimbursement obligations can be paid from the recovery. The final net amount depends on their real figures and terms; taxes may also raise case-specific questions.

Do not assume that every billed medical dollar becomes a recoverable lien or that the largest invoice is automatically the legally reasonable value of care. Ask for an itemized explanation of the gross amount, who is legally entitled to reimbursement, any reductions negotiated, the contractual fee calculation and the net distribution. When a government program is involved, appropriate notice and reimbursement procedures matter.

## Why a fair estimate changes as the file develops

Early assessment: a person has the police-report number and an initial medical visit, but fault and treatment duration are unclear. Mid-treatment assessment: medical notes, wage records and witness evidence are growing; there may still be unanswered prognosis questions. Resolution assessment: the relevant medical outlook, supported losses, evidence of responsibility, coverage and reimbursement issues are substantially clearer.

No stage authorizes a universal payment promise. A case can become stronger when new video identifies the responsible driver, or weaker when a purportedly permanent injury is not supported by medical evaluation. That is a reason to revisit assumptions as evidence develops, not proof that either claimant or insurer acted improperly.

## Questions to ask when someone quotes a case value

Ask what evidence supports the number, which kinds of damages were included, whether treatment and future-care assumptions are documented, how comparative fault was assessed, whether the number assumes more coverage than actually exists, and what would need to happen before you could reasonably receive money.

For original authority, consult the California Judicial Council's CACI damages instructions and the California Department of Insurance's current automobile insurance guide. An educational calculator can help organize facts, but it cannot replace a legally and medically supported assessment of a particular case.


## Compare settlement proposals on the same worksheet

Before comparing two offers, write down what each figure includes. Separate gross compensation from projected fees, litigation expenses, healthcare reimbursement claims and any other proposed deductions. Mark disputed or unknown deductions instead of silently treating them as zero. If a proposal depends on future information, identify which medical opinion, benefit record or coverage document is still missing.

Then compare the scope of the release, not only the number. Ask whose claims are being released, whether any claims remain, and how unresolved treatment or reimbursement issues will be handled. A calculator cannot evaluate release language or confirm what will be left after distribution. A useful case-value conversation should explain the assumptions behind the estimate and which changes in evidence would justify revisiting it.
